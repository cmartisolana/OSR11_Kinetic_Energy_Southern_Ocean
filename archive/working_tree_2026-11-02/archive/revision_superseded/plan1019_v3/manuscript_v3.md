**Surface kinetic energy trends of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, 2001–2025: anchored in Drake Passage observations**

[AUTHOR CHECK: working title from the editor's brief; alternatives in cover_note_v3.md]

Cristina Martí-Solana et al. (IMEDEA, CSIC–UIB)

**Abstract.** Eddy-resolving models and earlier altimetry suggest that stronger westerlies raise the eddy energy of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) more than its transport (eddy saturation). Earlier altimetric increases in eddy kinetic energy (EKE) were neither compared with in situ data nor examined with the mean flow and pathway. In Drake Passage, 1/8° altimetry reproduces the 2005–2019 interannual variability of ship-measured velocity (r = 0.72), EKE (annual r = 0.89) and transport north of 58.5°S (r = 0.67), but not of full-section transport. Over 2001–2025 the northern surface transport proxy has no detectable trend (+3.9 % per decade, p = 0.17) [PENDING: Antarctic-slope index clause], whereas upstream kinetic energy (KE) and EKE rise by 12.8 % and 15.3 % per decade in both products. Around the ACC, EKE rises by 8.0 % per decade (95 % interval 3.3–12.8; about half in a two-satellite product), KE by 4.9 %, along-stream speed by 2.0 % and the surface transport proxy by 1.6 %; the pathway position changes by −0.5 km per decade (−4.2 to +3.1). Over 35–65°S, EKE rises by 6.6 % per decade, of the order of earlier estimates. Eddies and mean flow strengthen together along an undisplaced pathway while transport proxies change several times less, consistent with, but not a test of, eddy saturation; surface-only data cannot separate equilibrium from slow adjustment.

# 1 Introduction

The Southern Ocean regulates the global uptake of heat and carbon and the exchange between the surface and deep layers (Gray, 2024). Its circulation is dominated by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), and much of its kinetic energy (KE) resides in mesoscale eddies, measured by the eddy kinetic energy (EKE) (Meredith and Hogg, 2006; Barceló-Llull et al., 2025; Cortés-Morales et al., 2026). The westerlies have intensified and shifted poleward, a positive trend of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM; Marshall, 2003), and the ACC response bears on projections of heat and carbon uptake, ice loss and overturning (Narayanan et al., 2026).

Coarse-resolution models predicted that stronger westerlies would accelerate the ACC (Hall and Visbeck, 2002; Saenko et al., 2005), but idealised and eddy-permitting models (Straub, 1993; Hallberg and Gnanadesikan, 2001, 2006), eddy-resolving experiments (Munday et al., 2013; Patara et al., 2016) and observations (Böning et al., 2008) show a much weaker sensitivity of the circumpolar transport (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Stewart et al., 2023; Maddison et al., 2025). In this regime, known as eddy saturation, stronger wind stress changes the time-mean transport little while energising transient eddies and standing meanders, so that EKE is expected to increase more than the mean flow and transport.

Several mechanisms may contribute. Transient eddies generated by baroclinic instability can release the potential energy added by the wind. At major topographic features, stronger forcing may instead enhance the downstream wakes through barotropic instability (Tansley and Marshall, 2001). In the standing-meander mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), stronger winds accelerate the flow upstream of a topographic constraint, the meander flexes, eddy fluxes and EKE increase downstream, and the jet slows again.

Altimetry has shown a circumpolar EKE response to a strong SAM event (Meredith and Hogg, 2006), EKE increases in the Indian and Pacific sectors (Hogg et al., 2015), period-dependent regional trends (Zhang et al., 2021) and intensifying mesoscale currents, with +5.1 % per decade south of 35°S over 1993–2020 (Martínez-Moreno et al., 2021). In Drake Passage, repeat shipboard ADCP transects show no significant trend in upper-ocean transport over 2005–2019, attributed to compensation among the frontal jets (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023). To our knowledge, the altimetric EKE increases have not been compared with in situ data, nor examined with the mean flow and pathway.

The Ocean State Report updates earlier evidence; an update is informative whether it confirms, weakens or changes earlier results. We ask whether, over 2001–2025, the surface eddy field intensified while the surface transport and pathway of the ACC changed little, and whether altimetry can extend the in situ record to answer this. Relative to earlier work, the analysis (i) compares altimetry with the Drake Passage ADCP record at matched spatial coverage, which defines what the altimetric record can extend; (ii) updates the estimates of Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) and Hogg et al. (2015) with a matched procedure, one autocorrelation-calibrated test and two products; (iii) examines the mean flow and tracked eddies alongside EKE; and (iv) bounds the displacement of the pathway with stated detectability and examines the surface ingredients of the standing-meander mechanism. We start in Drake Passage (Sect. 3.1–3.4) and then extend the analysis around the ACC (Sect. 3.5–3.7).

# 2 Data and methods

## 2.1 Products and definitions

Which surface quantities does altimetry provide, and at what scales? The primary product is the all-satellite DT2024 altimetry on a 1/8° grid (Product 1; Table 1); a two-satellite product on a 1/4° grid (Product 4) tests the dependence on the constellation (Supporting Information S1). The effective resolution is set by the mapping, not by the grid, and is of the order of 100–150 km in wavelength at ACC latitudes (Ballarotta et al., 2019) [CHECK: DT2024 effective resolution from the product QUID], so all quantities describe the surface geostrophic flow at the larger mesoscales. The record starts in 2001, when the constellation reached at least three missions (Cortés-Morales et al., 2026). No reanalysis or model output is used, because this observational chapter is built on the Copernicus products of Table 1 and model trends depend on their forcing; the adequacy of altimetry is instead tested against in situ data (Sect. 3.3).

## 2.2 Regions

Where is the signal sought? Drake Passage, the only place with an independent in situ record, is examined first, with a box over the passage (80–52°W, 63–54°S) and boxes within the ACC band upstream (100–80°W) and downstream (55–35°W). Pixels with ice fraction above 0.10 in any month are excluded (37 % of the box). Wind stress is averaged over the same longitudes at 63–54°S and over 45–60°S. The ACC band is the region where the 2001–2025 mean absolute dynamic topography (ADT) lies between −0.6 and +0.2 m, ice-free in every month, divided into Atlantic (70°W–20°E), Indian (20–147°E) and Pacific (147°E–70°W) sectors; the upstream box lies in the Pacific sector. Area means are cosine-weighted.

## 2.3 Half-power point

Has the energetic band moved? A trend in a fixed box could reflect a jet moving into or out of it. We locate it with the half-power point method of Chambers (2018), applied to the monthly-mean KE so that envelopes follow persistent jets rather than eddies. Unlike sea-surface height (SSH) contours, which shift poleward under sea-level rise (Gille, 2014), this amplitude-based position follows the energetic band; it does not test the transport, which a displacement changes only through the Coriolis parameter. In Drake Passage the profile follows the mean LM Gould track (Sect. 2.4); around the ACC, monthly envelopes are matched to the envelope of the mean field at each longitude, and only the 900 longitudes (about 31 % of the band) matched in at least 90 % of months are retained; the gaps, mainly in the Pacific where the band is weak and broad (including 115–75°W), are excluded. In synthetic tests, a 25 km shift over 25 years is detected in every realisation and a 10 km shift in 44 % (Supporting Information S3).

## 2.4 In situ record and comparison metrics

Does altimetry reproduce what is measured from ships? The in situ record (Product 3) consists of 248 ADCP transects of the ARSV Laurence M. Gould across Drake Passage over 2005–2019, in 25 km bins (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023): cross-track velocity at 70–118 m, EKE integrated over 0–780 m and total transport over 0–760 m. Altimetric values from the monthly field of each transect's month are compared along the mean track, per transect and as annual means. Because altimetry gives the surface geostrophic flow and the in situ quantities are taken at depth and include ageostrophic flow, the comparison is a consistency check of variability, not of magnitude. EKE and transport are compared north of about 58.5°S, where the section is ice-free in every month. The in situ band position is the latitude dividing the 0–760 m transport within the altimetric envelope into halves. Annual correlations are tested against surrogate series.

## 2.5 Transport quantities

Has the transport changed as the eddy field strengthened? Three quantities are kept distinct.

(a) The surface transport proxy (m² s⁻¹) integrates the surface geostrophic velocity normal to the mean LM Gould track over the 58 points north of 58.55°S that are ice-free in every month, because a section length varying with ice cover produces a spurious trend (Supporting Information S5). These points carry 67 % of the full-section mean proxy, and the in situ transport north of the limit 63 % of the in situ total: the proxy is a partial-section index. Around the ACC, the eastward surface velocity is integrated across the band. Neither version is a volume transport.

(b) The in situ transport is the total over 0–760 m (mean 75.5 Sv): a depth-independent part (51.1 Sv), an Ekman part (8.1 Sv) and the geostrophic transport relative to 760 m (15.9 Sv) (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023); it omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016) and is also split at the ice-free limit into northern and southern parts.

(c) The Antarctic-slope index follows Hughes et al. (2014) and Hogg et al. (2015), who related circumpolar transport anomalies to sea level over the Antarctic continental slope, most strongly at 1000–3000 m depth. We average the monthly sea-level anomaly of Product 1 over the points between the 1000 and 3000 m isobaths south of 60°S that are ice-free in that month, in 100 m depth bins and 12 longitude bands, recording the ice-free fraction. As in Hogg et al. (2015), the mean and the annual and semi-annual cycles are removed, a low-frequency part h_f is fitted with sinusoids of 10, 20 and 40 year periods, and the anomaly h (cm) is converted as

T = −3.69 (h − h_f) − 1.11 h_f (Sv cm⁻¹), [EQUATION 3] [CHECK: signs against Hogg et al., 2015]

with factors derived from NEMO model runs (Hughes et al., 2014) and called preliminary by Hogg et al. (2015). As these factors are the only model-derived element of the study, we report the index first and the transport, labelled model-calibrated, second. The conversion is least constrained at the lowest frequencies, which a trend represents, because sea level is amplified relative to bottom pressure beyond about 5 year periods (Hughes et al., 2014); steric or mass-driven sea-level trends at the margin alias into the index; and sea ice limits sampling (Supporting Information S7).

## 2.6 Along-stream path and tracked eddies

Is the change localised at standing meanders, and do they change shape? The jet path is tracked monthly as the +0.18 m contour of ADT relative to its monthly band mean, which removes sea-level rise (Gille, 2014); this level, near the Subantarctic Front, carries the highest mean speed. The path is smoothed at 150 km and divided into 72 bins of 5° of longitude, in which we compute its latitude, sinuosity (path length over zonal length, distinct from curvature), the speed along it and the EKE within 150 km. The two Malvinas Current bins (60–50°W) are excluded (Supporting Information S9); bins where the mean path crosses depths shallower than 3000 m mark the obstacles. Correlations between trend profiles are tested with 2000 circular shifts. Four wake boxes downstream of the Kerguelen Plateau, Southeast Indian Ridge–Macquarie Ridge, Pacific–Antarctic Ridge and Drake Passage, fixed before any trend was computed, test whether the EKE increase concentrates in the wakes. Tracked eddies (January 2001–August 2023) come from META4.0 (Product 5; Pegliasco et al., 2022), built from the all-satellite maps.

## 2.7 Statistics

How large must a trend be to be detected? Trends are Theil–Sen slopes of deseasonalised series (Theil, 1950; Sen, 1968), in absolute units and in percent of the period mean per decade. The slope is divided by its standard deviation under an autoregressive noise model with bootstrap bias-corrected coefficients (Kilian, 1998), and p-values and 95 % intervals are calibrated against 300 trend-free series simulated from that model. Monthly series are tested with AIC-selected and fixed (12) orders, reporting the larger p-value and the union of the intervals; annual series use AR(1). The false-positive rate at the nominal 5 % level is 3–5 % for monthly series and up to 9.5 % for 15 annual values (Supporting Information S2). A single level, p < 0.05, is used, and p = 0.050 is reported as at the threshold. For 1° cells and along-stream bins the false discovery rate (FDR) is controlled at 10 % (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Wilks, 2016). Contrasts (e.g. EKE − KE) are trends of differences of mean-normalised series. All regions and parameters were fixed before trends were computed, except the post hoc 400 cm² s⁻² Drake Passage run (Supporting Information S3).

# 3 Results

## 3.1 Drake Passage: energy, mean flow and wind

Has the eddy field intensified relative to the mean flow where the local forcing increased? An EKE increase with flat KE and along-stream speed would be consistent with eddy saturation; a mean-flow increase in proportion to the forcing, without an EKE change, would not.

Over 2001–2025 the eastward wind stress increases by 8.7 % per decade (1.4–15.9; p = 0.03) over the box and 9.8 % (1.3–18.4; p = 0.03) upstream, but not significantly downstream (+9.4 %, p = 0.09; Fig. 1b). In the box, KE (+2.4 % per decade, −2.5 to +7.4; p = 0.29) and the along-stream speed (−0.1 %, p = 0.91) show no detectable trend, while EKE increases by 8.4 % per decade (0.2–16.6; 21.7 cm² s⁻² per decade on a mean of 258 cm² s⁻²), at the threshold (p = 0.050). This trend is not confirmed by the two-satellite product and weakens without the ice mask (Supporting Information S1, S5), so no conclusion rests on it.

The clearest change lies upstream (Fig. 1b, c): KE increases by 12.8 % per decade (6.8–18.7; p = 0.007) and EKE by 15.3 % (6.2–24.4; p = 0.013), in both products (Supporting Information S1). The upstream mean is low, so the absolute changes, 19.2 (10.3–28.2) and 22.5 (9.1–36.0) cm² s⁻² per decade on means of 150 and 147 cm² s⁻², are comparable with the EKE change in the box. The upstream along-stream speed increases by 3.9 % per decade (0.3–7.5; 0.33 cm s⁻¹ per decade on a mean of 8.4 cm s⁻¹), at the threshold (p = 0.050). Downstream, EKE increases by 8.0 % per decade (3.0–13.0; p = 0.010) in the all-satellite product only, and KE shows no detectable trend (+4.3 %, p = 0.13). Inside the passage the pattern is thus at most weakly saturation-like, whereas upstream eddies and mean flow increase together with the wind, possibly as part of the Pacific-wide increase in along-stream speed (Sect. 3.6).

## 3.2 Position of the energetic band along the LM Gould section

Has the energetic band in the passage moved? A detectable shift would make the box trends ambiguous; a stationary band is required for an intensification in place but does not test eddy saturation.

Along the section, the climatological KE profile forms a single envelope between 55.1 and 59.3°S, because KE stays above 200 cm² s⁻² between the Subantarctic Front and the Polar Front. Its half-power point (57.0°S) is therefore the energy centroid of the merged band, not the position of a front. [AUTHOR CHECK: does the profile use points south of 58.5°S?] The band is detected in 99.7 % of months, and its position changes by −10 km per decade (−36 to +16; p = 0.43), a weak bound. Over 2005–2019 it follows the in situ half-transport latitude with r = 0.50 per transect and r = 0.71 for annual means (Fig. 2c), with a mean offset of 16 km and a root-mean-square (RMS) difference of 114 km, reflecting monthly fields compared with transects lasting about two days. A higher, post hoc threshold separates the two fronts, but the Polar Front is then detected in only 31 % of months (Supporting Information S3).

## 3.3 Altimetry compared with the in situ record

Does altimetry reproduce the in situ variability well enough to extend the record to 2001–2025 and beyond the section? It does not test eddy saturation but establishes which quantities can be carried forward.

Over 2005–2019 altimetry reproduces the mean cross-track velocity profile, the velocity per bin and transect (RMS difference 15 cm s⁻¹), EKE north of 58.5°S (annual r = 0.89) and transport north of 58.55°S (annual r = 0.67; surrogate p = 0.003) (Table 2; Fig. 2). The full-section transport is not reproduced (r = 0.03 per transect and for annual means), because the in situ transports north and south of the ice-free limit vary in opposition (r = −0.54). This opposition is partly built into the split, since a jet crossing a fixed latitude moves transport between the parts; it is consistent with the frontal compensation of Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) but does not test it. Trends cannot be checked: neither record has a significant trend over the 15 years, and the overlap ends in 2019, before the two altimetric products diverge (Supporting Information S1, S5).

## 3.4 Transport through Drake Passage

Has the transport changed as the eddy energy rose? A transport increase comparable to the EKE increase would be hard to reconcile with eddy saturation; no detectable trend would be consistent with it without testing it.

The northern surface transport proxy shows no detectable trend over 2001–2025 (Sect. 3.3; Fig. 3c). Over 2005–2019 the in situ 0–760 m total transport changes by −1.2 % per decade (p = 0.70), its northern part by −0.9 % (p = 0.87) and its southern part by +0.2 % (p = 0.98), from 15 annual values. ## 3.5 Circumpolar kinetic energy and comparison with earlier estimates

Is the Drake Passage pattern circumpolar, and how does it compare with earlier estimates? An EKE increase exceeding that of KE would be consistent with eddy saturation; comparable increases would indicate a joint intensification. Differences from earlier studies may arise from period, product and test as well as from the ocean.

Mean EKE and its increase are largest downstream of the Kerguelen Plateau, Southeast Indian Ridge and Campbell Plateau (Fig. 4a). Over the ACC band, KE increases by 1.36 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.86–1.86; 4.9 % per decade; p = 0.003) and EKE by 1.98 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.81–3.15; 8.0 % per decade; p = 0.017). In the two-satellite product the EKE trend is about half as large, although the difference between products is not significant (+4.3 points per decade, −2.2 to +10.8; p = 0.12; Supporting Information S1). KE and EKE trends are positive in all sectors, significant for both in the Pacific and Indian and for KE in the Atlantic (EKE p = 0.053); being relative to each sector's mean, they do not depend on sector size, and they do not support a ranking of sectors. Only 2.9 % (KE) and 4.0 % (EKE) of 1° cells reach p < 0.05, no more than expected by chance, and 5 and 18 cells survive FDR control: the signal rests on area averages. Interannual variability is not significantly correlated with the SAM (Supporting Information S10).

With the procedure of Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) (EKE integrated over 35–65°S, 12-month running mean, Theil–Sen slope), our data give +5.2 % per decade over 2001–2020 and +7.5 % over 2001–2025, the same order as their +5.1 % south of 35°S over 1993–2020 with an earlier product version; our integrated EKE level is about twice theirs, for reasons not yet investigated (Supporting Information S6). With the calibrated test the trend is +4.5 % per decade (p = 0.13) over 2001–2020 and +6.6 % (1.2–12.0; p = 0.04) over 2001–2025, and with the ice mask +4.6 % (p = 0.11) and +6.6 % (1.5–11.6; p = 0.03) (Fig. 4b). Over this domain the extension to 2025 makes the increase detectable with a conservative test; over the ACC band it is already significant over 2001–2020 (+6.4 %, p = 0.04). In the boxes of Hogg et al. (2015), EKE increases over 2001–2025 by 9.0 % per decade (4.9–13.0; p = 0.007) in the Indian and 8.6 % (0.9–16.4; p = 0.04) in the Pacific sector, against their 6.0 ± 1.6 % and 6.4 ± 1.8 % per decade (crossover altimetry, relative to a 1993–2001 mean); the smaller two-satellite trends mean that the two products bracket the earlier values (Supporting Information S1). With different periods, sampling and reference means, this is consistency, not reproduction.

## 3.6 Mean flow, surface transport proxy and eddies

Is the added energy found in the eddies rather than in the mean flow? An EKE trend clearly exceeding those of the mean flow and the surface transport proxy would be consistent with eddy saturation; comparable trends would indicate a joint intensification that surface data cannot attribute.

The along-stream speed increases by 2.0 % per decade over the band (p = 0.003; about 2 % in both products), by 3.5 % in the Pacific and 1.8 % in the Atlantic, but not significantly in the Indian sector (Fig. 4c). The circumpolar surface transport proxy increases by 1.6 % per decade (0.9–2.3; p = 0.003): 2.6 % in the Pacific, 1.8 % in the Atlantic and 0.4 % (not significant) in the Indian sector. The excess of the relative EKE trend over the KE trend, +3.0 % per decade (−0.1 to +6.1; p = 0.060), is not significant. The EKE trend is about five times that of the surface transport proxy in the all-satellite product and about 2.5 times in the two-satellite product (Supporting Information S1); this ratio describes the surface partition but does not test eddy saturation.

About 345 tracked eddies are present per day (mean amplitude 7.7 cm, speed radius 41 km). Over January 2001–August 2023 their number (+0.8 % per decade, p = 0.37) and radius (−0.1 %, p = 0.60) show no detectable trend, whereas their amplitude increases by 4.6 % per decade (2.4–6.8; p = 0.007) and their rotational speed by 3.8 % (0.9–6.8; p = 0.02) (Fig. 4d). Within the resolved range, the EKE increase thus reflects stronger rather than more numerous eddies; as the radius is limited by the mapping (fifth percentile 24 km), its stability is weakly informative (Supporting Information S8).

## 3.7 Pathway and standing meanders

Has the pathway moved, and are the surface ingredients of the standing-meander mechanism detectable? A fixed-band trend could reflect a moving pathway, and the mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014) implies upstream acceleration, meander flexing and EKE increases localised downstream of topography; detecting these would make it a candidate explanation, while their absence at the surface would leave it untested.

The circumpolar-mean half-power latitude changes by −0.5 km per decade (−4.2 to +3.1; p = 0.74), whereas a sustained 25 km shift over the record would have been detected; no sector or 5° bin has a significant trend (Fig. 5a; Supporting Information S3). Trends over the regions of earlier studies are also small, e.g. −1.6 km per decade (−12.9 to +9.6) at 90–170°E and +5.6 (−4.9 to +16.2) at 160–90°W, where Chambers (2018) found −29 and +46 km per decade over 1993–2016, and an order of magnitude below the southward shifts of SSH contours (about 40 km per decade over 1992–2007; Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b). The methods differ; one possibility is that the earlier regional shifts belonged to the earlier period. Schiavon et al. (2025) likewise found no significant jet-position trends, with mainly interannual variability over flat topography.

Along the jet-core path, EKE increases in 58 of 70 bins (Fig. 5b), by similar amounts over the obstacles (7.0 % per decade on average), in the three bins downstream (7.3 %) and elsewhere (6.9 %); 12 bins reach p < 0.05 and none survives FDR control. The four wake boxes hold 43 % of the band's mean EKE and 45 % of its increase (relative-trend difference +0.1 % per decade, p = 0.95). The jet speed increases in 51 of 70 bins (median +3.9 % per decade; Fig. 5c). At the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge, on the sections of Liu et al. (2026), the jet core accelerates by 0.019 m s⁻¹ per decade (p = 0.01), whereas upstream of the ridge the increase is borderline (p = 0.06). Neither the sinuosity (36 of 70 bins positive; 3 with p < 0.05, none after FDR control) nor the path latitude (35 of 70 positive; 3 isolated bins after control) shows a tendency (Fig. 5d, e). A half-power curvature test at individual meanders was feasible only at the Southeast Indian Ridge, where a 25 km flexing would not be detectable (Supporting Information S4). Across bins, the EKE trend is larger where the jet-speed trend is larger (r = 0.40, p = 0.008); because EKE and the monthly path share the eddies and respond to common mapping changes, this association does not separate cause from effect.

# 4 Discussion and conclusions

The Drake Passage comparison licenses extending EKE, velocity and the northern surface transport, but not the transport of the seasonally ice-covered southern part (Table 2); it does not check trends, because the overlap is 15 years long and ends in 2019, before the products diverge. Within these limits, the passage shows no detectable change in transport indicators or band position, and robust energy increases only upstream.

Reading a flat transport as insensitivity to wind requires the forcing to have changed. The wind stress increased by about 9 % per decade over and upstream of Drake Passage, but the transport responds to the circumpolar stress, whose trend over 45–60°S (+2.5 % per decade, −0.3 to +5.3; p = 0.08) is not detected. The absence of a detectable trend in the Drake Passage transport indicators is therefore consistent with eddy saturation (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Maddison et al., 2025) but does not test it; it is also consistent with compensation among the frontal jets (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023), which the north–south opposition of the in situ transport echoes without testing. [PENDING: does the Antarctic-slope index agree with the in situ record and with the weak decline indicated by Hogg et al. (2015)?]

Eddy saturation concerns the depth-integrated transport. The strengthening of the surface mean flow around the ACC (along-stream speed +2.0 %, surface transport proxy +1.6 % per decade) neither confirms nor contradicts it: the surface proxies do not represent the flow at depth, the in situ record omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016), and KE includes slow meander variability. The surface record shows eddies and mean flow intensifying together, the EKE increase not significantly exceeding that of KE. A weak transport trend over 25 years is also not unique to an eddy-saturated equilibrium: Böning et al. (2008) found little change in the ACC isopycnal slope despite stronger westerlies, and the ACC and the global pycnocline adjust to sustained forcing over multidecadal to centennial time scales (Allison et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2011; Samelson, 2011). The record cannot separate an equilibrated saturated state from slow adjustment.

Of the links in the mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), the surface record shows parts of the initial link, upstream acceleration: upstream of Drake Passage KE increases in both products but the along-stream speed only at the threshold, and at the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge the jet core accelerates within the meander but only borderline upstream. As the upstream Drake box lies in the Pacific, where speed increases sector-wide, this acceleration may not be meander-specific. Flexing is not detected in sinuosity or path latitude, and a 25 km flexing would not be detectable with half-power positions; the EKE increase is not concentrated over or downstream of the obstacles. The final link, a slowing of the jet, is not observed: the surface jet speed increases, and the record cannot isolate a compensating slowing at depth or within individual meanders. The uniform increase does not favour barotropic instability in the wakes either (Tansley and Marshall, 2001); without subsurface data, baroclinic-instability indicators cannot be computed, and the surface diagnostics cannot distinguish these pathways, whose localising steps may occur below the surface or at unresolved scales.

Relative to Hogg et al. (2015) and Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021), the update confirms the sign and order of the EKE increase with a different reprocessing, period and test; its magnitude depends on the constellation by about a factor of two, a difference not significant over 25 years but large enough to quote both products. It adds the in situ comparison, the mean flow examined with the eddies, and a bound on pathway displacement with stated detectability.

Several limitations remain. Altimetry resolves the surface geostrophic flow at wavelengths of about 100–150 km at these latitudes, so the small-scale end of the mesoscale band and the vertical structure of EKE are not observed. The Antarctic-slope index rests on model-derived conversion factors and on the sea-ice sampling of the slope [PENDING: ice-free fraction]. Extending the in situ comparison beyond 2019 would show whether the post-2018 constellation changes affect the extended record, and observations from the Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission may help resolve smaller surface scales.

In summary, anchored by the comparison in Drake Passage, the altimetric record shows that over 2001–2025 the surface eddy field and mean flow of the ACC strengthened together along a pathway with no detectable displacement, while surface transport proxies changed several times less and the Drake Passage transport indicators showed no detectable trend. This pattern is consistent with eddy saturation but does not test it, because the circumpolar wind-stress trend is not detected, the full-depth transport is unobserved and the record cannot separate equilibrium from slow adjustment. Whether the pattern persists as the record lengthens remains open.
