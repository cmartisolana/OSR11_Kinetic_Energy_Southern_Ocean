**Eddies and mean flow of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current intensified together at the surface, 2001–2025**

[AUTHOR CHECK: impact-oriented working title; alternative: "Surface eddy and mean-flow trends of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, 2001–2025"]

Cristina Martí-Solana et al. (IMEDEA, CSIC–UIB)

**Abstract.** Stronger westerlies are expected to energise the eddies of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) more than its transport (eddy saturation). Using 1/8° satellite altimetry over 2001–2025, we find that the surface eddy kinetic energy (EKE) of the ACC increased by 8.0 % per decade (95 % interval 3.3–12.8), carried by stronger rather than more numerous eddies, while the surface mean flow also strengthened (along-stream speed +2.0 % per decade). The earlier altimetric EKE increase persists in the reprocessed record: over 35–65°S, EKE rises by 6.6 % per decade, of the order of earlier estimates. The energetic pathway shows no detectable displacement where it is persistently tracked (−0.5 km per decade, −4.2 to +3.1), and the EKE increase is not concentrated downstream of topography. The largest changes lie upstream of Drake Passage, where kinetic energy and EKE rise by 12.8 % and 15.3 % per decade, while the Drake Passage transport indicators show no detectable trend [PENDING: Antarctic-slope index clause]. The EKE increase is about half as large in a two-satellite product. With no detected circumpolar wind-stress trend and surface-only data, these changes are compatible with eddy saturation but do not discriminate it from a joint intensification of the surface flow.

# 1 Introduction

The Southern Ocean regulates the global uptake of heat and carbon and the exchange between the surface and deep layers (Gray, 2024). Its circulation is dominated by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), and much of its kinetic energy (KE) resides in mesoscale eddies, measured by the eddy kinetic energy (EKE) (Meredith and Hogg, 2006; Barceló-Llull et al., 2025; Cortés-Morales et al., 2026). The westerlies have intensified and shifted poleward, a positive trend of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM; Marshall, 2003), and the ACC response bears on projections of heat and carbon uptake, ice loss and overturning (Narayanan et al., 2026).

Coarse-resolution models predicted that stronger westerlies would accelerate the ACC (Hall and Visbeck, 2002; Saenko et al., 2005), but idealised and eddy-permitting models (Straub, 1993; Hallberg and Gnanadesikan, 2001, 2006) and eddy-resolving experiments (Munday et al., 2013; Patara et al., 2016), and observed isopycnal slopes (Böning et al., 2008), indicate a much weaker sensitivity of the circumpolar transport (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Stewart et al., 2023; Maddison et al., 2025). In this regime, known as eddy saturation, stronger wind stress changes the time-mean transport little while energising transient eddies and standing meanders, so that EKE is expected to increase more than the mean flow and transport.

Several mechanisms may contribute. Transient eddies generated by baroclinic instability can release the potential energy added by the wind. At major topographic features, stronger forcing may instead enhance the downstream wakes through barotropic instability (Tansley and Marshall, 2001). In the standing-meander mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), stronger winds accelerate the flow upstream of a topographic constraint, the meander flexes, eddy fluxes and EKE increase downstream, and the jet slows again.

Altimetry has shown a circumpolar EKE response to a strong SAM event (Meredith and Hogg, 2006), EKE increases in the Indian and Pacific sectors alongside a transport index from Antarctic-slope sea level (Hogg et al., 2015), period-dependent regional trends (Zhang et al., 2021), intensifying mesoscale currents, with +5.1 % per decade south of 35°S over 1993–2020 (Martínez-Moreno et al., 2021), and pathway shifts (Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b; Chambers, 2018). In Drake Passage, repeat ADCP transects show no significant trend in net 0–760 m transport over 2005–2019, with opposing trends among frontal regions, and an EKE increase between the Subantarctic and Polar Fronts consistent with altimetric EKE trends and with eddy saturation (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023). These eddy, mean-flow, transport and pathway changes have, however, been estimated in separate studies with different periods, products and tests, so it remains unclear whether the eddy field has intensified relative to the mean flow, and whether it has done so in place.

We ask whether, over 2001–2025, the surface eddy field of the ACC intensified while its surface transport and pathway changed little. Relative to earlier work, the analysis (i) updates the estimates of Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) and Hogg et al. (2015) with their procedure and with an autocorrelation-calibrated test, in two altimetry products; (ii) examines the mean flow, surface transport proxies and tracked eddies alongside EKE; (iii) bounds the displacement of the pathway with stated detectability and examines the surface ingredients of the standing-meander mechanism; and (iv) resolves Drake Passage, where in situ transport is available, into the passage and the regions upstream and downstream of it. As an Ocean State Report update, the result is informative whether it confirms or qualifies earlier estimates. We start in Drake Passage (Sect. 3.1) and then extend the analysis around the ACC (Sects. 3.2–3.4).

# 2 Data and methods

## 2.1 Products and definitions

The primary product is the all-satellite DT2024 altimetry on a 1/8° grid (Product 1; Table 1); a two-satellite product on a 1/4° grid (Product 4) tests the dependence on the constellation (Supporting Information S1). The effective resolution, set by the mapping rather than the grid, is of the order of 100–150 km in wavelength at ACC latitudes (Ballarotta et al., 2019) [CHECK: DT2024 effective resolution from the product QUID]. Reanalysis and model output are not used, apart from the published model-derived factors of Sect. 2.4(c). Altimetric velocity, EKE and northern transport were compared with the Drake Passage ADCP record of Product 3 in Supporting Information S5.

## 2.2 Regions

Drake Passage is examined with a box over the passage (80–52°W, 63–54°S) and boxes within the ACC band upstream (100–80°W) and downstream (55–35°W). Pixels with ice fraction above 0.10 in any month are excluded (37 % of the box). Wind stress is averaged over the same longitudes at 63–54°S and over 45–60°S. The ACC band is the region where the 2001–2025 mean absolute dynamic topography (ADT) lies between −0.6 and +0.2 m, ice-free in every month, divided into Atlantic (70°W–20°E), Indian (20–147°E) and Pacific (147°E–70°W) sectors; the upstream box lies in the Pacific sector. Area means are cosine-weighted.

## 2.3 Half-power point

We locate the energetic band with the half-power point method of Chambers (2018), applied to the monthly-mean KE so that envelopes follow persistent jets rather than eddies. Unlike sea-surface height (SSH) contours, it is not shifted by sea-level rise (Gille, 2014); it does not test the transport, which a displacement changes only through the Coriolis parameter. The threshold is set to 200 cm² s⁻², the rounded median of the 2001–2025 mean KE over the ACC band (195 cm² s⁻²). As a KE-weighted median, φhp also responds to unequal intensification of the flanks, so it bounds the displacement of the energy distribution rather than of a jet axis. Monthly envelopes are matched to the mean envelope at each longitude; only the 900 longitudes (about 31 % of the band) matched in at least 90 % of months are kept, and the excluded gaps lie mainly in the Pacific, including 115–75°W, where the band is weak and broad. In synthetic tests φhp recovers about 70 % of an imposed shift; a 25 km shift over 25 years is detected in every realisation and a 10 km shift in 44 % (sensitivity and synthetic tests in Supporting Information S3).

## 2.4 Transport quantities

Because they measure different things, three transport quantities are kept distinct.

(a) The surface transport proxy (m² s⁻¹) integrates the surface geostrophic velocity normal to the mean track of the LM Gould ADCP transects across Drake Passage over the 58 points north of 58.55°S that are ice-free in every month, because a section length varying with ice cover produces a spurious trend (Supporting Information S5). These points carry 67 % of the full-section mean proxy: the proxy is a partial-section index. Around the ACC, the eastward surface velocity is integrated across the band. Neither version is a volume transport.

(b) The in situ transport is the total over 0–760 m from 248 ADCP transects of the ARSV Laurence M. Gould over 2005–2019 (Product 3; Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023) [AUTHOR CHECK: Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) quote 237 transects for EKE], with a mean of 75.5 Sv: a depth-independent part (51.1 Sv), an Ekman part (8.1 Sv) and the geostrophic transport relative to 760 m (15.9 Sv). It omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016).

(c) The Antarctic-slope index follows Hughes et al. (2014) and Hogg et al. (2015), who related circumpolar transport anomalies to sea level over the Antarctic continental slope. We average the monthly sea-level anomaly of Product 1 over the points of the 1000–3000 m depth range of the continental slope contiguous with the Antarctic shelf, excluding isolated rises, plateaus and ridges (e.g. Maud Rise, the southern Kerguelen Plateau, the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge), that are ice-free in that month. As in Hogg et al. (2015), the mean and the annual and semi-annual cycles are removed, a low-frequency part h_f is fitted with sinusoids of 10, 20 and 40 year periods, and the anomaly h (cm) is converted as

T = −3.69 (h − h_f) − 1.11 h_f (Sv cm⁻¹), [EQUATION 3] [CHECK: signs against Hogg et al., 2015]

with factors derived from NEMO model runs (Hughes et al., 2014) and called preliminary by Hogg et al. (2015). Being the only model-derived quantity of the study, the transport is reported after the index and labelled model-calibrated. The conversion is least constrained for trends, and margin sea-level change and sea-ice sampling may alias into the index (Supporting Information S7).

## 2.5 Along-stream path and tracked eddies

The jet path is tracked monthly as the +0.18 m contour of ADT relative to its monthly band mean, which removes the band-mean part of sea-level rise (Gille, 2014). The path is smoothed at 150 km and divided into 72 bins of 5° of longitude, in which we compute its latitude, sinuosity (path length over zonal length, distinct from curvature), the speed along it and the EKE within 150 km. The two Malvinas Current bins (60–50°W) are excluded (Supporting Information S9); bins where the mean path crosses depths shallower than 3000 m mark the obstacles. Correlations between trend profiles are tested with 2000 circular shifts. Four wake boxes downstream of major topography (Fig. 2a) test whether the EKE increase concentrates in the wakes. Tracked eddies come from META4.0 (Product 5; Table 1).

## 2.6 Statistics

Trends are Theil–Sen slopes of deseasonalised series (Theil, 1950; Sen, 1968), in absolute units and in percent of the period mean per decade. The slope is divided by its standard deviation under an autoregressive noise model with bootstrap bias-corrected coefficients (Kilian, 1998), and p-values and 95 % intervals are calibrated against 300 trend-free series simulated from that model. Monthly series take the more conservative of two model orders and annual series use AR(1); false-positive rates at the nominal 5 % level are 3–5 % for monthly series and up to 9.5 % for 15 annual values (Supporting Information S2). A single level, p < 0.05, is used; p = 0.050 is reported as at the threshold, and p of about 0.03–0.07 as marginal, because with 300 null series p near 0.05 carries a Monte Carlo uncertainty of about ±0.01. For 1° cells and along-stream bins the false discovery rate (FDR) is controlled at 10 %, equivalent to a global level of about 5 % for correlated fields (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Wilks, 2016). Contrasts (e.g. EKE − KE) are trends of differences of mean-normalised series, in percentage points per decade. Because EKE and KE scale with velocity squared and the speed and transport proxies with velocity, we also compare them in energy-equivalent terms (twice the relative trend of a velocity-like quantity). All regions and parameters were fixed before trends were computed; the few post hoc tests are listed in Supporting Information S2.

# 3 Results

## 3.1 Drake Passage: energy, wind and transport

Has the eddy field intensified relative to the mean flow where the local forcing increased? An EKE increase with flat KE, along-stream speed and transport would be consistent with eddy saturation; a mean-flow or transport increase in proportion to the forcing would not.

Over 2001–2025 the eastward wind stress increases by 8.7 % per decade (1.4–15.9; p = 0.03) over the box and 9.8 % (1.3–18.4; p = 0.03) upstream, but not significantly downstream (+9.4 %, p = 0.09; Fig. 1b). The clearest oceanic change lies upstream (Fig. 1b, c): KE increases by 12.8 % per decade (6.8–18.7; p = 0.007) and EKE by 15.3 % (6.2–24.4; p = 0.013), in both products (Supporting Information S1). The upstream mean is low, so the absolute changes, 19.2 (10.3–28.2) and 22.5 (9.1–36.0) cm² s⁻² per decade on means of 150 and 147 cm² s⁻², are comparable with the EKE change in the box. The upstream along-stream speed increases by 3.9 % per decade (0.3–7.5; 0.33 cm s⁻¹ per decade on a mean of 8.4 cm s⁻¹), at the threshold (p = 0.050).

Inside the passage, KE (+2.4 % per decade, −2.5 to +7.4; p = 0.29) and the along-stream speed (−0.1 %, p = 0.91) show no detectable trend, while EKE increases by 8.4 % per decade (0.2–16.6; 21.7 cm² s⁻² per decade on a mean of 258 cm² s⁻²), at the threshold (p = 0.050); this trend is not confirmed by the two-satellite product and weakens without the ice mask (Supporting Information S1, S5), so no conclusion rests on it. Downstream, EKE increases by 8.0 % per decade (3.0–13.0; p = 0.010) in the all-satellite product only, and KE shows no detectable trend (+4.3 %, p = 0.13). The energetic band along the LM Gould section, where the Subantarctic and Polar Fronts merge into one envelope, shows no detectable displacement (−10 km per decade, −36 to +16; Supporting Information S3).

The transport indicators show no detectable trend (Fig. 1b). The northern surface transport proxy changes by +3.9 % per decade over 2001–2025 (−2.1 to +9.9; p = 0.17), and the in situ 0–760 m transport by −1.2 % per decade over 2005–2019 (p = 0.70), from 15 annual values. As energy scales with velocity squared, the proxy interval does not exclude an energy-equivalent change comparable to that of box EKE. Inside the passage the pattern thus matches the eddy-saturation expectation only weakly, whereas upstream eddies and mean flow increase with the wind, possibly as part of the Pacific-wide increase in along-stream speed (Sect. 3.3).

## 3.2 Circumpolar EKE increase and earlier estimates

Is the Drake Passage pattern circumpolar, and does the earlier altimetric EKE increase persist? Differences from earlier studies may arise from period, product and test as well as from the ocean.

Over the ACC band, EKE increases by 1.98 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.81–3.15; 8.0 % per decade; p = 0.017) and KE by 1.36 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.86–1.86; 4.9 % per decade; p = 0.003). In the two-satellite product the EKE trend is about half as large, although the difference between products is not significant (+4.3 percentage points per decade, −2.2 to +10.8; p = 0.12; Supporting Information S1). KE and EKE trends are positive in all sectors, significant for both in the Pacific and Indian and for KE in the Atlantic (EKE p = 0.053). Relative trends do not scale with area, but their significance can, because a larger sector averages more independent variability; they do not support a ranking of sectors. The largest absolute increases, mostly not significant at the cell level, lie where mean EKE is largest, downstream of the Kerguelen Plateau, Southeast Indian Ridge and Campbell Plateau (Fig. 2a). Only 2.9 % (KE) and 4.0 % (EKE) of 1° cells reach p < 0.05, no more than expected by chance, and 5 and 18 cells survive FDR control: the signal rests on area averages.

With the procedure of Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) applied to EKE integrated over 35–65°S, our data give +5.2 % per decade over 2001–2020 and +7.5 % over 2001–2025, the same order as their +5.1 % over 1993–2020. Our integrated EKE level is about twice theirs, for reasons not yet investigated (Supporting Information S6). With the calibrated test the trend is +4.5 % per decade (p = 0.13) over 2001–2020 and +6.6 % (1.2–12.0; p = 0.04) over 2001–2025 (Fig. 2b); this gain in detectability with the extension to 2025 is marginal (Sect. 2.6). In the boxes of Hogg et al. (2015), EKE increases over 2001–2025 by 9.0 % per decade (4.9–13.0; p = 0.007) in the Indian and 8.6 % (0.9–16.4; p = 0.04) in the Pacific sector, against their 6.0 ± 1.6 % and 6.4 ± 1.8 % per decade; the smaller two-satellite trends mean that the two products bracket the earlier values (Supporting Information S1). With different periods, sampling and reference means, this is consistency, not reproduction.

## 3.3 Mean flow, surface transport proxy and eddies

Is the added energy found in the eddies rather than in the mean flow? An EKE trend clearly exceeding, in like terms, those of the mean flow and the surface transport proxy would be consistent with eddy saturation; comparable trends would indicate a joint intensification that surface data cannot attribute.

The along-stream speed increases by 2.0 % per decade over the band (p = 0.003; about 2 % in both products), by 3.5 % in the Pacific and 1.8 % in the Atlantic, but not significantly in the Indian sector (Fig. 2c). The circumpolar surface transport proxy increases by 1.6 % per decade (0.9–2.3; p = 0.003): 2.6 % in the Pacific, 1.8 % in the Atlantic and 0.4 % (not significant) in the Indian sector. The excess of the relative EKE trend over the KE trend, +3.0 percentage points per decade (−0.1 to +6.1; p = 0.060), is not significant. The ratio of the relative EKE and proxy trends is 5.0 in the all-satellite and 2.5 in the two-satellite product; in energy-equivalent terms, however, the circumpolar proxy rises at about 40 % of the EKE rate in the all-satellite product (3.2 against 8.0 % per decade) and at a comparable rate in the two-satellite product (3.0 against 3.8 %; Supporting Information S1), and the along-stream speed (4.0 %) at about the KE rate (4.9 %). This describes the surface partition and is not a saturation test.

About 345 tracked eddies are present per day (mean amplitude 7.7 cm, speed radius 41 km). Over January 2001–August 2023 their number (+0.8 % per decade, p = 0.37) and radius (−0.1 %, p = 0.60) show no detectable trend, whereas their amplitude increases by 4.6 % per decade (2.4–6.8; p = 0.007) and their rotational speed by 3.8 % (0.9–6.8; p = 0.02) (Fig. 2d). Within the resolved range, the EKE increase thus reflects stronger rather than more numerous eddies; as the radius is limited by the mapping (fifth percentile 24 km), its stability is weakly informative, and the atlas, built from the all-satellite maps, shares their constellation dependence (Supporting Information S1, S8).

## 3.4 Pathway and standing meanders

Has the pathway moved, and are the surface ingredients of the standing-meander mechanism detectable? A fixed-band trend could reflect a moving pathway. The mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014) implies upstream acceleration, meander flexing and EKE increases localised downstream of topography. Detecting these at the surface would make it a candidate explanation; not detecting them would leave it untested.

Averaged over the 900 persistently tracked longitudes, the half-power latitude changes by −0.5 km per decade (−4.2 to +3.1; p = 0.74), although a 25 km shift would have been detected; no sector has a significant trend, and none of the 33 bins of 5° reaches p < 0.05 (Fig. 3a; Supporting Information S3). Trends over the regions of earlier studies are also small, e.g. −1.6 km per decade (−12.9 to +9.6) at 90–170°E and +5.6 (−4.9 to +16.2) at 160–90°W, where Chambers (2018) found −29 and +46 km per decade over 1993–2016. Our trends are an order of magnitude below the southward shifts of SSH contours (about 40 km per decade over 1992–2007; Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b). Schiavon et al. (2025) likewise found no significant jet-position trends, with mainly interannual variability over flat topography.

Along the jet-core path, EKE increases in 58 of 70 bins (Fig. 3b), by similar amounts over the obstacles (7.0 % per decade on average), in the three bins downstream (7.3 %) and elsewhere (6.9 %); 12 bins reach p < 0.05 and none survives FDR control. The four wake boxes hold 43 % of the band's mean EKE and 45 % of its increase (relative-trend difference +0.1 percentage points per decade, p = 0.95). The jet speed increases in 51 of 70 bins (median +4.1 % per decade; Fig. 3c). At the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge, on the sections of Liu et al. (2026), the jet core accelerates by 0.019 m s⁻¹ per decade (p = 0.01), whereas upstream of the ridge the increase is borderline (p = 0.06). Neither the sinuosity (36 of 70 bins positive; 3 with p < 0.05, none after FDR control) nor the path latitude (35 of 70 positive; 3 isolated bins after control) shows a tendency (Fig. 3d, e). A half-power curvature test at individual meanders was feasible only at the Southeast Indian Ridge, where a 25 km flexing would not be detectable (Supporting Information S4). Across bins, the EKE trend is larger where the jet-speed trend is larger (r = 0.40, p = 0.008); because EKE and the monthly path share the eddies and respond to common mapping changes, this association does not separate cause from effect.

# 4 Discussion and conclusions

Three results emerge from the 2001–2025 altimetric record. First, the surface EKE of the ACC increased by 8 % per decade, carried by stronger rather than more numerous eddies, and the earlier altimetric increase (Hogg et al., 2015; Martínez-Moreno et al., 2021) persists in the reprocessed record with a conservative test. Second, the surface mean flow strengthened as well, so the record shows a joint intensification rather than eddies growing over an unchanged mean flow. Third, the energetic pathway has not moved detectably where it is persistently tracked, consistent with an intensification in place and without the regional shifts reported for 1993–2016 (Chambers, 2018), a difference that may reflect the period or the method. The magnitude of the EKE increase depends on the constellation by about a factor of two, a difference not significant over 25 years but large enough to quote both products.

These results bear on how the ACC is monitored. Surface EKE has become a detectable trend indicator over the 25-year record, but its magnitude depends on the satellite constellation, so its monitoring needs a constant-constellation product alongside the all-satellite one. Because the mean flow strengthened together with the eddies, the eddy field alone would miss part of the surface response; the along-stream speed and the pathway position, bounded to a few kilometres per decade where tracked, are complementary indicators.

The wind stress increased by about 9 % per decade over and upstream of Drake Passage, but the transport responds to the circumpolar stress, whose trend over 45–60°S (+2.5 % per decade, −0.3 to +5.3; p = 0.08) is not detected. The absence of a detectable trend in the Drake Passage transport indicators is therefore consistent with eddy saturation (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Maddison et al., 2025) but does not test it, and the proxy interval does not exclude a change comparable in energy terms to that of EKE. For the same reason, the EKE increase cannot be attributed to the circumpolar wind on this evidence: under eddy saturation, eddy energy scales roughly with the stress (Marshall et al., 2017 [verify]; Maddison et al., 2025), but the circumpolar EKE trend (8.0 % per decade; 3.8 % in the two-satellite product) is larger than the undetected stress trend (2.5 %), and EKE is not significantly correlated with the SAM (Supporting Information S10). [PENDING: comparison with Hogg et al. (2015)]

Eddy saturation concerns the depth-integrated transport. The strengthening of the surface mean flow neither confirms nor contradicts it: the surface proxies do not represent the flow at depth, a larger steric sea-level rise north of the ACC would raise the circumpolar proxy without changing the depth-integrated transport, the in situ record omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016), and KE includes slow meander variability. The EKE increase does not significantly exceed that of KE, and the eddy–transport contrast is modest and product-dependent in energy-equivalent terms (Sect. 3.3). A weak transport trend over 25 years is also not unique to an eddy-saturated equilibrium: the ACC and the global pycnocline adjust to sustained forcing over multidecadal to centennial time scales (Allison et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2011; Samelson, 2011), which the record cannot separate from an equilibrated saturated state.

Of the links in the mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), the surface record shows parts of the first, upstream acceleration: upstream of Drake Passage KE and EKE increase in both products, and at the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge the jet core accelerates within the meander, but in both places the upstream speed increase is only at the threshold or borderline, and the upstream Drake region lies in the Pacific, where speed increases sector-wide. Flexing is not detected and, at 25 km, would not be detectable; the EKE increase is not concentrated over or downstream of the obstacles. Under slowly increasing forcing the final link would appear as compensation limiting the net acceleration rather than as an absolute slowing; the surface jet speed increases, so any compensation is at most partial at the surface, and the record cannot isolate it at depth or within meanders. The uniform increase does not favour barotropic instability in the wakes either (Tansley and Marshall, 2001); without subsurface data, baroclinic-instability indicators cannot be computed, and the surface diagnostics cannot distinguish these pathways.

Altimetry resolves the surface geostrophic flow at wavelengths of about 100–150 km at these latitudes, so the small-scale end of the mesoscale band and the vertical structure of EKE are not observed. The Antarctic-slope index rests on model-derived conversion factors and on the sea-ice sampling of the slope [PENDING: ice-free fraction]. Observations from the Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission may help resolve smaller surface scales.

In summary, over 2001–2025 the surface eddy field and mean flow of the ACC strengthened together, with no detectable displacement where the energetic band is persistently tracked, while the Drake Passage transport indicators showed no detectable trend. These observations are compatible with eddy saturation but do not discriminate it from a joint intensification of the surface flow, because the circumpolar wind-stress trend is not detected, the eddy–transport contrast is small in like terms and product-dependent, the full-depth transport is unobserved, and the record cannot separate equilibrium from slow adjustment. Whether the pattern persists as the record lengthens remains open.
