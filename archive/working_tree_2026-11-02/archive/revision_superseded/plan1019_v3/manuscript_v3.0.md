**Surface eddy and mean-flow trends of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, 2001–2025, from altimetry compared with Drake Passage observations**

[AUTHOR CHECK: title per the editor's decision on the v3 reviews; alternatives in cover_note_v3.1.md]

Cristina Martí-Solana et al. (IMEDEA, CSIC–UIB)

**Abstract.** Eddy-resolving models suggest stronger westerlies raise the eddy kinetic energy (EKE) of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) more than its transport (eddy saturation). Altimetric and in situ variability have not, to our knowledge, been compared at matched coverage, and eddy, mean-flow and pathway changes were estimated in separate studies. We compare altimetry with Drake Passage ADCP transects (2005–2019) and extend the record to 2001–2025 around the ACC. Altimetry reproduces velocity anomalies (r = 0.61 per bin and transect) and the interannual variability of in situ EKE (annual r = 0.89) and transport north of 58.55°S (annual r = 0.67), but not full-section transport. The northern surface transport proxy has no detectable trend [PENDING: Antarctic-slope index clause]. ACC-wide, EKE rises by 8.0 % per decade (about half with two satellites), kinetic energy by 4.9 %, along-stream speed by 2.0 % and the surface transport proxy by 1.6 %, with no detectable displacement where the energetic band is persistently tracked (31 % of longitudes). Over 35–65°S, the EKE trend (6.6 % per decade) is of the order of earlier estimates. With no detected circumpolar wind-stress trend, these observations are compatible with eddy saturation but do not discriminate it from a joint intensification of the surface flow.

# 1 Introduction

The Southern Ocean regulates the global uptake of heat and carbon and the exchange between the surface and deep layers (Gray, 2024). Its circulation is dominated by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), and much of its kinetic energy (KE) resides in mesoscale eddies, measured by the eddy kinetic energy (EKE) (Meredith and Hogg, 2006; Barceló-Llull et al., 2025; Cortés-Morales et al., 2026). The westerlies have intensified and shifted poleward, a positive trend of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM; Marshall, 2003), and the ACC response bears on projections of heat and carbon uptake, ice loss and overturning (Narayanan et al., 2026).

Coarse-resolution models predicted that stronger westerlies would accelerate the ACC (Hall and Visbeck, 2002; Saenko et al., 2005), but idealised and eddy-permitting models (Straub, 1993; Hallberg and Gnanadesikan, 2001, 2006) and eddy-resolving experiments (Munday et al., 2013; Patara et al., 2016), and observed isopycnal slopes (Böning et al., 2008), indicate a much weaker sensitivity of the circumpolar transport (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Stewart et al., 2023; Maddison et al., 2025). In this regime, known as eddy saturation, stronger wind stress changes the time-mean transport little while energising transient eddies and standing meanders, so that EKE is expected to increase more than the mean flow and transport.

Several mechanisms may contribute. Transient eddies generated by baroclinic instability can release the potential energy added by the wind. At major topographic features, stronger forcing may instead enhance the downstream wakes through barotropic instability (Tansley and Marshall, 2001). In the standing-meander mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), stronger winds accelerate the flow upstream of a topographic constraint, the meander flexes, eddy fluxes and EKE increase downstream, and the jet slows again.

Altimetry has shown a circumpolar EKE response to a strong SAM event (Meredith and Hogg, 2006), EKE increases in the Indian and Pacific sectors alongside a transport index from Antarctic-slope sea level (Hogg et al., 2015), period-dependent regional trends (Zhang et al., 2021), intensifying mesoscale currents, with +5.1 % per decade south of 35°S over 1993–2020 (Martínez-Moreno et al., 2021), and pathway shifts (Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b; Chambers, 2018). In Drake Passage, repeat ADCP transects show no significant trend in net 0–760 m transport over 2005–2019, with opposing trends among frontal regions, and an EKE increase between the Subantarctic and Polar Fronts consistent with altimetric EKE trends and with eddy saturation (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023). To our knowledge, altimetric and in situ variability have not been compared at matched coverage to define what the altimetric record can extend, and eddy, mean-flow, transport and pathway changes have been estimated in separate studies with different periods, products and tests.

We ask whether, over 2001–2025, the surface eddy field intensified while the surface transport and pathway of the ACC changed little, and whether altimetry can extend the in situ record to answer this. Relative to earlier work, the analysis (i) compares altimetry with the Drake Passage ADCP record at matched spatial coverage; (ii) repeats the estimates of Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) and Hogg et al. (2015) with their procedure and with an autocorrelation-calibrated test, in two altimetry products; (iii) examines the mean flow and tracked eddies alongside EKE; and (iv) bounds the displacement of the pathway with stated detectability and examines the surface ingredients of the standing-meander mechanism. As an Ocean State Report update, the result is informative whether it confirms or qualifies earlier estimates. We start in Drake Passage, where the energy trends and band position are set against the in situ record and the transport indicators (Sects. 3.1–3.4), and then follow the quantities that the comparison supports around the ACC (Sects. 3.5–3.7).

# 2 Data and methods

## 2.1 Products and definitions

The primary product is the all-satellite DT2024 altimetry on a 1/8° grid (Product 1; Table 1); a two-satellite product on a 1/4° grid (Product 4) tests the dependence on the constellation (Supporting Information S1). The effective resolution, set by the mapping rather than the grid, is of the order of 100–150 km in wavelength at ACC latitudes (Ballarotta et al., 2019) [CHECK: DT2024 effective resolution from the product QUID]. The record starts in 2001, when the constellation reached at least three missions (Cortés-Morales et al., 2026). Reanalysis and model output are not used, apart from the published model-derived factors of Sect. 2.5(c), because their trends depend on their forcing; the adequacy of altimetry is tested against in situ data instead (Sect. 3.3).

## 2.2 Regions

Drake Passage is examined with a box over the passage (80–52°W, 63–54°S) and boxes within the ACC band upstream (100–80°W) and downstream (55–35°W). Pixels with ice fraction above 0.10 in any month are excluded (37 % of the box). Wind stress is averaged over the same longitudes at 63–54°S and over 45–60°S. The ACC band is the region where the 2001–2025 mean absolute dynamic topography (ADT) lies between −0.6 and +0.2 m, ice-free in every month, divided into Atlantic (70°W–20°E), Indian (20–147°E) and Pacific (147°E–70°W) sectors; the upstream box lies in the Pacific sector. Area means are cosine-weighted.

## 2.3 Half-power point

We locate the energetic band with the half-power point method of Chambers (2018), applied to the monthly-mean KE so that envelopes follow persistent jets rather than eddies. Unlike sea-surface height (SSH) contours, it is not shifted by sea-level rise (Gille, 2014); it does not test the transport, which a displacement changes only through the Coriolis parameter. Along each KE profile, segments above 200 cm² s⁻² are identified, each envelope extends to where KE falls to 25 % of its peak (minimum width 100 km), and the half-power latitude φhp divides the KE integrated across the envelope into halves:

The threshold is set to 200 cm² s⁻², the rounded median of the 2001–2025 mean KE over the ACC band (195 cm² s⁻²), so that envelopes are the parts of the band more energetic than its typical level. As a KE-weighted median, φhp also responds to unequal intensification of the flanks, so it bounds the displacement of the energy distribution rather than of a jet axis. In Drake Passage the profile follows the mean LM Gould track (Sect. 2.4). Around the ACC, monthly envelopes are matched to the mean envelope at each longitude. Only the 900 longitudes (about 31 % of the band) matched in at least 90 % of months are kept; the excluded gaps lie mainly in the Pacific, including 115–75°W, where the band is weak and broad. In synthetic tests φhp recovers about 70 % of an imposed shift; a 25 km shift over 25 years is detected in every realisation and a 10 km shift in 44 % (sensitivity and synthetic tests in Supporting Information S3).

## 2.4 In situ record and comparison metrics

The in situ record comprises 248 ADCP transects of the ARSV Laurence M. Gould across Drake Passage over 2005–2019 (Product 3; Table 1; Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023): cross-track velocity at 70–118 m, EKE integrated over 0–780 m (the archive variable, which Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) present depth-averaged; the scaling does not affect correlations) and total transport over 0–760 m [AUTHOR CHECK: Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) quote 237 transects for EKE]. Altimetric values from the monthly field of each transect's month are compared along the mean track, per transect and as annual means. As altimetry gives surface geostrophic flow and the in situ quantities include deeper and ageostrophic flow, the comparison checks variability, not magnitude. EKE and transport are compared north of about 58.5°S, ice-free in every month. The in situ band position is the latitude dividing the 0–760 m transport within the climatological altimetric envelope into halves, so no shared variability is built in. Annual correlations are tested against surrogate series.

## 2.5 Transport quantities

Because they measure different things, three transport quantities are kept distinct.

(a) The surface transport proxy (m² s⁻¹) integrates the surface geostrophic velocity normal to the mean LM Gould track over the 58 points north of 58.55°S that are ice-free in every month, because a section length varying with ice cover produces a spurious trend (Supporting Information S5). These points carry 67 % of the full-section mean proxy, and the in situ transport north of the limit 63 % of the in situ total: the proxy is a partial-section index. Around the ACC, the eastward surface velocity is integrated across the band. Neither version is a volume transport.

(b) The in situ transport is the total over 0–760 m (mean 75.5 Sv): a depth-independent part (51.1 Sv), an Ekman part (8.1 Sv) and the geostrophic transport relative to 760 m (15.9 Sv) (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023); it omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016) and is also split at the ice-free limit into northern and southern parts.

(c) The Antarctic-slope index follows Hughes et al. (2014) and Hogg et al. (2015), who related circumpolar transport anomalies to sea level over the Antarctic continental slope. We average the monthly sea-level anomaly of Product 1 over the points of the 1000–3000 m depth range of the continental slope contiguous with the Antarctic shelf, excluding isolated rises, plateaus and ridges (e.g. Maud Rise, the southern Kerguelen Plateau, the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge), that are ice-free in that month. As in Hogg et al. (2015), the mean and the annual and semi-annual cycles are removed, a low-frequency part h_f is fitted with sinusoids of 10, 20 and 40 year periods, and the anomaly h (cm) is converted as

T = −3.69 (h − h_f) − 1.11 h_f (Sv cm⁻¹), [EQUATION 3] [CHECK: signs against Hogg et al., 2015]

with factors derived from NEMO model runs (Hughes et al., 2014) and called preliminary by Hogg et al. (2015). Being the only model-derived quantity of the study, the transport is reported after the index and labelled model-calibrated. The conversion is least constrained for trends, and margin sea-level change and sea-ice sampling may alias into the index (Supporting Information S7).

## 2.6 Along-stream path and tracked eddies

The jet path is tracked monthly as the +0.18 m contour of ADT relative to its monthly band mean, which removes the band-mean part of sea-level rise (Gille, 2014). The path is smoothed at 150 km and divided into 72 bins of 5° of longitude, in which we compute its latitude, sinuosity (path length over zonal length, distinct from curvature), the speed along it and the EKE within 150 km. The two Malvinas Current bins (60–50°W) are excluded (Supporting Information S9); bins where the mean path crosses depths shallower than 3000 m mark the obstacles. Correlations between trend profiles are tested with 2000 circular shifts. Four wake boxes downstream of major topography (Fig. 4a) test whether the EKE increase concentrates in the wakes. Tracked eddies come from META4.0 (Product 5; Table 1).

## 2.7 Statistics

Trends are Theil–Sen slopes of deseasonalised series (Theil, 1950; Sen, 1968), in absolute units and in percent of the period mean per decade. The slope is divided by its standard deviation under an autoregressive noise model with bootstrap bias-corrected coefficients (Kilian, 1998), and p-values and 95 % intervals are calibrated against 300 trend-free series simulated from that model. Monthly series take the more conservative of two model orders and annual series use AR(1); false-positive rates at the nominal 5 % level are 3–5 % for monthly series and up to 9.5 % for 15 annual values (Supporting Information S2). A single level, p < 0.05, is used; p = 0.050 is reported as at the threshold, and p of about 0.03–0.07 as marginal, because with 300 null series p near 0.05 carries a Monte Carlo uncertainty of about ±0.01. For 1° cells and along-stream bins the false discovery rate (FDR) is controlled at 10 %, equivalent to a global level of about 5 % for correlated fields (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Wilks, 2016). Contrasts (e.g. EKE − KE) are trends of differences of mean-normalised series, in percentage points per decade. All regions and parameters were fixed before trends were computed; the few post hoc tests are listed in Supporting Information S2.

# 3 Results

## 3.1 Drake Passage: energy, mean flow and wind

Has the eddy field intensified relative to the mean flow where the local forcing increased? An EKE increase with flat KE and along-stream speed would be consistent with eddy saturation; a mean-flow increase in proportion to the forcing, without an EKE change, would not.

Over 2001–2025 the eastward wind stress increases by 8.7 % per decade (1.4–15.9; p = 0.03) over the box and 9.8 % (1.3–18.4; p = 0.03) upstream, but not significantly downstream (+9.4 %, p = 0.09; Fig. 1b). In the box, KE (+2.4 % per decade, −2.5 to +7.4; p = 0.29) and the along-stream speed (−0.1 %, p = 0.91) show no detectable trend, while EKE increases by 8.4 % per decade (0.2–16.6; 21.7 cm² s⁻² per decade on a mean of 258 cm² s⁻²), at the threshold (p = 0.050). This trend is not confirmed by the two-satellite product and weakens without the ice mask (Supporting Information S1, S5), so no conclusion rests on it.

The clearest change lies upstream (Fig. 1b, c): KE increases by 12.8 % per decade (6.8–18.7; p = 0.007) and EKE by 15.3 % (6.2–24.4; p = 0.013), in both products (Supporting Information S1). The upstream mean is low, so the absolute changes, 19.2 (10.3–28.2) and 22.5 (9.1–36.0) cm² s⁻² per decade on means of 150 and 147 cm² s⁻², are comparable with the EKE change in the box. The upstream along-stream speed increases by 3.9 % per decade (0.3–7.5; 0.33 cm s⁻¹ per decade on a mean of 8.4 cm s⁻¹), at the threshold (p = 0.050). Downstream, EKE increases by 8.0 % per decade (3.0–13.0; p = 0.010) in the all-satellite product only, and KE shows no detectable trend (+4.3 %, p = 0.13). Inside the passage the pattern thus matches the eddy-saturation expectation only weakly; upstream, the speed increase at the threshold may belong to the Pacific-wide increase (Sect. 3.6).

## 3.2 Position of the energetic band along the LM Gould section

Has the energetic band in the passage moved? A detectable shift would make the box trends ambiguous; a stationary band is required for an intensification in place but does not test eddy saturation.

Along the section, the climatological KE profile forms a single envelope between 55.1 and 59.3°S, because KE stays above 200 cm² s⁻² between the Subantarctic Front and the Polar Front. Its half-power point (57.0°S) is therefore the energy median of the merged band, not the position of a front. Points with ice fraction above 0.10 are masked monthly, so points south of 58.5°S enter only when ice-free. The band is detected in 99.7 % of months, and its position changes by −10 km per decade (−36 to +16; p = 0.43), a weak bound. Over 2005–2019 it follows the in situ half-transport latitude with r = 0.50 per transect and r = 0.71 for annual means (0.50 with the profile truncated at the ice-free limit, for an unchanged trend; Supporting Information S3) (Fig. 2c), with a mean offset of 16 km and a root-mean-square (RMS) difference of 114 km, reflecting monthly fields compared with transects lasting about two days.

## 3.3 Altimetry compared with the in situ record

Does altimetry reproduce the in situ variability well enough to extend the record at this section to 2001–2025 and, by plausibility rather than test, to other ice-free parts of the ACC? This is a prerequisite, not a test of eddy saturation.

Over 2005–2019 altimetry reproduces the mean cross-track velocity profile, the velocity per bin and transect (RMS difference 15 cm s⁻¹; anomalies r = 0.61), EKE north of 58.5°S (annual r = 0.89) and transport north of 58.55°S (annual r = 0.67; surrogate p = 0.003) (Table 2; Fig. 2). The full-section in situ transport is reproduced neither by the northern proxy (r = 0.03 per transect and for annual means), as the in situ transports north and south of the ice-free limit vary in opposition (r = −0.54), nor by the whole-line integral including values mapped under seasonal ice (r = 0.07 per transect; Supporting Information S5). This opposition is partly built into the split (a jet crossing a fixed latitude moves transport between the parts) and does not test the opposing frontal trends of Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023). Trends cannot be checked: over 15 annual values the in situ intervals (EKE −58.8 to +74.4 % per decade) are many times wider than the trends to be extended, and the overlap ends in 2019, as the products begin to diverge (Supporting Information S1, S5).

## 3.4 Transport through Drake Passage

Has the transport changed as the eddy energy rose? A transport increase comparable in energy-equivalent terms to the EKE increase would be hard to reconcile with eddy saturation; no detectable trend would be consistent with it without testing it.

Over 2001–2025 the northern surface transport proxy changes by +3.9 % per decade (−2.1 to +9.9; p = 0.17), not detectably (Fig. 2d, e). As energy scales with velocity squared, this interval does not exclude an energy-equivalent change comparable to that of box EKE (Sect. 3.6). Over 2005–2019 the in situ 0–760 m total transport changes by −1.2 % per decade (p = 0.70), its northern part by −0.9 % (p = 0.87) and its southern part by +0.2 % (p = 0.98), from 15 annual values. ## 3.5 Circumpolar kinetic energy and comparison with earlier estimates

Is the Drake Passage pattern circumpolar, and how does it compare with earlier estimates? An EKE increase exceeding that of KE would be consistent with eddy saturation; comparable increases would indicate a joint intensification.

The largest absolute increases, mostly not significant at the cell level, lie where mean EKE is largest, downstream of the Kerguelen Plateau, Southeast Indian Ridge and Campbell Plateau (Fig. 4a). Over the ACC band, KE increases by 1.36 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.86–1.86; 4.9 % per decade; p = 0.003) and EKE by 1.98 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.81–3.15; 8.0 % per decade; p = 0.017). In the two-satellite product the EKE trend is about half as large, although the difference between products is not significant (+4.3 percentage points per decade, −2.2 to +10.8; p = 0.12; Supporting Information S1). KE and EKE trends are positive in all sectors, significant for both in the Pacific and Indian and for KE in the Atlantic (EKE p = 0.053). Relative trends do not scale with area, but their significance can, because a larger sector averages more independent variability; they do not support a ranking of sectors. Only 2.9 % (KE) and 4.0 % (EKE) of 1° cells reach p < 0.05, no more than expected by chance, and 5 and 18 cells survive FDR control: the signal rests on area averages.

## 3.6 Mean flow, surface transport proxy and eddies

Is the added energy found in the eddies rather than in the mean flow? An EKE trend clearly exceeding, in like terms, those of the mean flow and the surface transport proxy would be consistent with eddy saturation; comparable trends would indicate a joint intensification that surface data cannot attribute.

The along-stream speed increases by 2.0 % per decade over the band (p = 0.003; about 2 % in both products), by 3.5 % in the Pacific and 1.8 % in the Atlantic, but not significantly in the Indian sector (Fig. 4c). The circumpolar surface transport proxy increases by 1.6 % per decade (0.9–2.3; p = 0.003): 2.6 % in the Pacific, 1.8 % in the Atlantic and 0.4 % (not significant) in the Indian sector. The excess of the relative EKE trend over the KE trend, +3.0 percentage points per decade (−0.1 to +6.1; p = 0.060), is not significant. The ratio of the relative EKE and proxy trends is 5.0 in the all-satellite and 2.5 in the two-satellite product, but energy scales with velocity squared (Sect. 3.4): in energy-equivalent terms (twice the relative trend of a velocity-like quantity), the circumpolar proxy rises at about 40 % of the EKE rate in the all-satellite product (3.2 against 8.0 % per decade) and at a comparable rate in the two-satellite product (3.0 against 3.8 %; Supporting Information S1), the along-stream speed (4.0 %) at about the KE rate (4.9 %), and in Drake Passage the northern proxy (7.8 %) at a rate comparable with box EKE (8.4 %). This describes the surface partition and is not a saturation test.

About 345 tracked eddies are present per day (mean amplitude 7.7 cm, speed radius 41 km). Over January 2001–August 2023 their number (+0.8 % per decade, p = 0.37) and radius (−0.1 %, p = 0.60) show no detectable trend, whereas their amplitude increases by 4.6 % per decade (2.4–6.8; p = 0.007) and their rotational speed by 3.8 % (0.9–6.8; p = 0.02) (Fig. 4d). Within the resolved range, the EKE increase thus reflects stronger rather than more numerous eddies; as the radius is limited by the mapping (fifth percentile 24 km), its stability is weakly informative, and the atlas, built from the all-satellite maps, shares their constellation dependence (Supporting Information S1, S8).

## 3.7 Pathway and standing meanders

Has the pathway moved, and are the surface ingredients of the standing-meander mechanism detectable? A fixed-band trend could reflect a moving pathway. The mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014) implies upstream acceleration, meander flexing and EKE increases localised downstream of topography. Detecting these at the surface would make it a candidate explanation; not detecting them would leave it untested.

Averaged over the 900 persistently tracked longitudes, the half-power latitude changes by −0.5 km per decade (−4.2 to +3.1; p = 0.74), although a 25 km shift would have been detected; no sector has a significant trend, and none of the 33 bins of 5° reaches p < 0.05 (revised test; Fig. 5a; Supporting Information S3). Trends over the regions of earlier studies are also small, e.g. −1.6 km per decade (−12.9 to +9.6) at 90–170°E and +5.6 (−4.9 to +16.2) at 160–90°W, where Chambers (2018) found −29 and +46 km per decade over 1993–2016. Our trends are an order of magnitude below the southward shifts of SSH contours (about 40 km per decade over 1992–2007; Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b). Schiavon et al. (2025) likewise found no significant jet-position trends, with mainly interannual variability over flat topography.

Along the jet-core path, EKE increases in 58 of 70 bins (Fig. 5b), by similar amounts over the obstacles (7.0 % per decade on average), in the three bins downstream (7.3 %) and elsewhere (6.9 %); 12 bins reach p < 0.05 and none survives FDR control. The four wake boxes hold 43 % of the band's mean EKE and 45 % of its increase (relative-trend difference +0.1 percentage points per decade, p = 0.95). The jet speed increases in 51 of 70 bins (median +4.1 % per decade; Fig. 5c). At the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge, on the sections of Liu et al. (2026), the jet core accelerates by 0.019 m s⁻¹ per decade (p = 0.01), whereas upstream of the ridge the increase is borderline (p = 0.06). Neither the sinuosity (36 of 70 bins positive; 3 with p < 0.05, none after FDR control) nor the path latitude (35 of 70 positive; 3 isolated bins after control) shows a tendency (Fig. 5d, e). A half-power curvature test at individual meanders was feasible only at the Southeast Indian Ridge, where a 25 km flexing would not be detectable (Supporting Information S4). Across bins, the EKE trend is larger where the jet-speed trend is larger (r = 0.40, p = 0.008); because EKE and the monthly path share the eddies and respond to common mapping changes, this association does not separate cause from effect.

# 4 Discussion and conclusions

The Drake Passage comparison licenses extending the interannual variability of EKE, velocity anomalies and northern transport at that section, not trends, and elsewhere only by plausibility (Table 2). Within these limits, the passage shows no detectable change in transport indicators or band position, and robust energy increases only upstream; the northern proxy interval does not, however, exclude an energy-equivalent change comparable to that of box EKE.

The wind stress increased by about 9 % per decade over and upstream of Drake Passage, but the transport responds to the circumpolar stress, whose trend over 45–60°S (+2.5 % per decade, −0.3 to +5.3; p = 0.08) is not detected. The absence of a detectable trend in the Drake Passage transport indicators is therefore consistent with eddy saturation (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Maddison et al., 2025) but does not test it. For the same reason, the EKE increase cannot be attributed to the circumpolar wind on this evidence: under eddy saturation, eddy energy scales roughly with the stress (Marshall et al., 2017 [verify]; Maddison et al., 2025), but the circumpolar EKE trend (8.0 % per decade; 3.8 % in the two-satellite product) is larger than the undetected stress trend (2.5 %), and EKE is not significantly correlated with the SAM (Supporting Information S10). [PENDING: comparison with Hogg et al. (2015)]

Eddy saturation concerns the depth-integrated transport. The strengthening of the surface mean flow around the ACC neither confirms nor contradicts it: the surface proxies do not represent the flow at depth, a larger steric sea-level rise north of the ACC would raise the circumpolar proxy without changing the depth-integrated transport, the in situ record omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016), and KE includes slow meander variability. The surface record shows eddies and mean flow intensifying together: the EKE increase does not significantly exceed that of KE, and the eddy–transport contrast is modest and product-dependent in energy-equivalent terms (Sect. 3.6). A weak transport trend over 25 years is also not unique to an eddy-saturated equilibrium: the ACC and the global pycnocline adjust to sustained forcing over multidecadal to centennial time scales (Allison et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2011; Samelson, 2011), which the record cannot separate from an equilibrated saturated state.

Of the links in the mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), the surface record shows parts of the first, upstream acceleration, which may not be meander-specific given the Pacific-wide speed increase (Sects. 3.1, 3.7). Flexing is not detected and, at 25 km, would not be detectable; the EKE increase is not concentrated over or downstream of the obstacles. Under slowly increasing forcing the final link would appear as compensation limiting the net acceleration rather than as an absolute slowing; the surface jet speed increases, so any compensation is at most partial at the surface, and the record cannot isolate it at depth or within meanders. The uniform increase does not favour barotropic instability in the wakes either (Tansley and Marshall, 2001); without subsurface data, baroclinic-instability indicators cannot be computed, and the surface diagnostics cannot distinguish these pathways.

Relative to Hogg et al. (2015) and Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021), the update confirms the sign and order of the EKE increase with a different reprocessing, period and test; its magnitude depends on the constellation by about a factor of two, a difference not significant over 25 years but large enough to quote both products. It also qualifies earlier results: the regional pathway shifts reported for 1993–2016 (Chambers, 2018) are not found over 2001–2025, which may reflect the period or the method, and the EKE increase is accompanied by a detectable strengthening of the surface mean flow.

Altimetry resolves the surface geostrophic flow at wavelengths of about 100–150 km at these latitudes, so the small-scale end of the mesoscale band and the vertical structure of EKE are not observed. The Antarctic-slope index rests on model-derived conversion factors and on the sea-ice sampling of the slope [PENDING: ice-free fraction]. Extending the in situ comparison beyond 2019 would show whether the post-2018 constellation changes affect the extended record, and observations from the Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission may help resolve smaller surface scales.

In summary, altimetry reproduces transect-scale velocity anomalies and the interannual variability of EKE and northern transport across Drake Passage. Around the ACC, the altimetric record shows that over 2001–2025 the surface eddy field and mean flow strengthened together, with no detectable displacement where the energetic band is persistently tracked, while the Drake Passage transport indicators showed no detectable trend. These observations are compatible with eddy saturation but do not discriminate it from a joint intensification of the surface flow, because the circumpolar wind-stress trend is not detected, the eddy–transport contrast is small in like terms and product-dependent, the full-depth transport is unobserved, and the record cannot separate equilibrium from slow adjustment. Whether the pattern persists as the record lengthens remains open.
