**Eddies and mean flow of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current intensified together at the surface, 2001–2025**

**Key points.** [AUTHOR CHECK: not part of the State of the Planet template; for the cover letter, short summary and OSR synthesis]

- The surface eddy kinetic energy of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current rose by about 8 % per decade over 2001–2025, because existing eddies became stronger, not more numerous.
- The surface mean flow accelerated as well, by about 2 % per decade, while the energetic pathway stayed in place: the current intensified rather than migrating south.
- The in situ Drake Passage transport shows no detectable trend [PENDING: Antarctic-slope index], and the energetic band stays on a fixed streamline; with surface-only data, eddy saturation is compatible with the record but not tested by it.

**Abstract.** The Southern Hemisphere westerlies have strengthened, and theory predicts that the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC) responds through its eddies more than through its transport (eddy saturation). We examine its surface expression with 1/8° satellite altimetry over 2001–2025. The surface eddy kinetic energy (EKE) of the ACC rose by 8.0 % per decade (95 % interval 3.3–12.8; about half as large in a two-satellite product), because existing eddies became stronger, not more numerous. The surface mean flow accelerated too (along-stream speed +2.0 % per decade), and the energetic pathway did not shift detectably (−0.5 km per decade, −4.2 to +3.1): the current intensified in place rather than migrating south. The largest changes lie upstream of Drake Passage, where kinetic energy and EKE increased by 13–15 % per decade. Within the passage, the flow accelerated between the Subantarctic and Polar Fronts while the surface transport between streamlines barely changed, consistent with more vigorous meandering, and the in situ transport shows no detectable trend over 2005–2019 [PENDING: Antarctic-slope index clause]. Because the data are surface-only and the transport is not constrained after 2019 [PENDING], these changes are compatible with eddy saturation but do not distinguish it from a joint intensification of eddies and mean flow.

# 1 Introduction

The Southern Ocean regulates the global uptake of heat and carbon and the exchange between the surface and deep layers (Gray, 2024). Its circulation is dominated by the Antarctic Circumpolar Current (ACC), and much of its kinetic energy (KE) resides in mesoscale eddies, measured by the eddy kinetic energy (EKE) (Meredith and Hogg, 2006; Barceló-Llull et al., 2025; Cortés-Morales et al., 2026). The westerlies have intensified and shifted poleward, a positive trend of the Southern Annular Mode (SAM; Marshall, 2003), and the ACC response bears on projections of heat and carbon uptake, ice loss and overturning (Narayanan et al., 2026).

Coarse-resolution models predicted that stronger westerlies would accelerate the ACC (Hall and Visbeck, 2002; Saenko et al., 2005), but idealised and eddy-permitting models (Straub, 1993; Hallberg and Gnanadesikan, 2001, 2006) and eddy-resolving experiments (Munday et al., 2013; Patara et al., 2016), and observed isopycnal slopes (Böning et al., 2008), indicate a much weaker sensitivity of the circumpolar transport (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Stewart et al., 2023; Maddison et al., 2025). In this regime, known as eddy saturation, stronger wind stress changes the time-mean transport little while energising transient eddies and standing meanders, so that EKE is expected to increase more than the mean flow and transport.

Several mechanisms may contribute. Transient eddies generated by baroclinic instability can release the potential energy added by the wind. At major topographic features, stronger forcing may instead enhance the downstream wakes through barotropic instability (Tansley and Marshall, 2001). In the standing-meander mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), stronger winds accelerate the flow upstream of a topographic constraint, the meander flexes, eddy fluxes and EKE increase downstream, and the jet slows again.

Altimetry has shown a circumpolar EKE response to a strong SAM event (Meredith and Hogg, 2006), EKE increases in the Indian and Pacific sectors alongside a transport index from Antarctic-slope sea level (Hogg et al., 2015), period-dependent regional trends (Zhang et al., 2021), intensifying mesoscale currents, with +5.1 % per decade south of 35°S over 1993–2020 (Martínez-Moreno et al., 2021), and pathway shifts (Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b; Chambers, 2018). In Drake Passage, repeat ADCP transects show no significant trend in net 0–760 m transport over 2005–2019, with opposing trends among frontal regions that largely vanish in streamwise coordinates, where mainly the Subantarctic Front accelerated, and an EKE increase between the Subantarctic and Polar Fronts consistent with altimetric EKE trends and with eddy saturation (Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023). These eddy, mean-flow, transport and pathway changes have, however, been estimated in separate studies with different periods, products and tests, so it remains unclear whether the eddy field has intensified relative to the mean flow, and whether it has done so in place.

We ask whether, over 2001–2025, the surface eddy field of the ACC intensified while its transport and pathway changed little. Relative to earlier work, the analysis (i) updates the estimates of Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) and Hogg et al. (2015) with their procedure and with an autocorrelation-calibrated test, in two altimetry products; (ii) examines the mean flow, an Antarctic-slope transport index and tracked eddies alongside EKE; (iii) bounds the displacement of the pathway with stated detectability and examines the surface ingredients of the standing-meander mechanism; and (iv) resolves Drake Passage into the passage and the regions upstream and downstream of it, and compares the changes along the LM Gould transect with the shipboard record of Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023). As an Ocean State Report update, the result is informative whether it confirms or qualifies earlier estimates. We start in Drake Passage (Sect. 3.1) and then extend the analysis around the ACC (Sects. 3.2–3.5).

# 2 Data and methods

## 2.1 Products and definitions

The primary product is the all-satellite DT2024 altimetry on a 1/8° grid (Product 1; Table 1); a two-satellite product on a 1/4° grid (Product 4) tests the dependence on the constellation (Supporting Information S1). The effective resolution, set by the mapping rather than the grid, is of the order of 100–150 km in wavelength at ACC latitudes (Ballarotta et al., 2019) [CHECK: DT2024 effective resolution from the product QUID]. The record starts in 2001, when the constellation reached at least three missions (Cortés-Morales et al., 2026). Reanalysis and model output are not used, apart from the published model-derived factors of Sect. 2.4(c). Altimetric velocity, EKE and northern transport were compared with the Drake Passage ADCP record of Product 3 in Supporting Information S5.

## 2.2 Regions

Drake Passage is examined with a box over the passage (80–52°W, 63–54°S) and boxes within the ACC band upstream (100–80°W) and downstream (55–35°W). Pixels with ice fraction above 0.10 in any month are excluded (37 % of the box). The ACC band is the region where the 2001–2025 mean absolute dynamic topography (ADT) lies between −0.6 and +0.2 m, ice-free in every month, divided into Atlantic (70°W–20°E), Indian (20–147°E) and Pacific (147°E–70°W) sectors; the upstream box lies in the Pacific sector. Area means are cosine-weighted.

## 2.3 Half-power point

We locate the energetic band with the half-power point method of Chambers (2018), applied to the monthly-mean KE so that envelopes follow persistent jets rather than eddies. Unlike sea-surface height (SSH) contours, it is not shifted by sea-level rise (Gille, 2014); it does not test the transport, which a displacement changes only through the Coriolis parameter. The threshold is set to 200 cm² s⁻², the rounded median of the 2001–2025 mean KE over the ACC band (195 cm² s⁻²). As a KE-weighted median, φhp also responds to unequal intensification of the flanks, so it bounds the displacement of the energy distribution rather than of a jet axis. Monthly envelopes are matched to the mean envelope at each longitude; only the 900 longitudes (about 31 % of the band) matched in at least 90 % of months are kept, and the excluded gaps lie mainly in the Pacific, including 115–75°W, where the band is weak and broad. In synthetic tests φhp recovers about 70 % of an imposed shift; a 25 km shift over 25 years is detected in every realisation and a 10 km shift in 44 % (Supporting Information S3).

## 2.4 Transport quantities

Two transport quantities are used.

(a) The in situ transport is the total over 0–760 m from 248 ADCP transects of the ARSV Laurence M. Gould over 2005–2019 (Product 3; Gutierrez-Villanueva et al., 2023) [AUTHOR CHECK: Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) quote 237 transects for EKE], with a mean of 75.5 Sv: a depth-independent part (51.1 Sv), an Ekman part (8.1 Sv) and the geostrophic transport relative to 760 m (15.9 Sv). It omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016).

(b) The Antarctic-slope index follows Hughes et al. (2014) and Hogg et al. (2015), who related circumpolar transport anomalies to sea level over the Antarctic continental slope. We average the monthly sea-level anomaly of Product 1 over the points of the 1000–3000 m depth range of the continental slope contiguous with the Antarctic shelf, excluding isolated rises, plateaus and ridges (e.g. Maud Rise, the southern Kerguelen Plateau, the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge), that are ice-free in that month. As in Hogg et al. (2015), the mean and the annual and semi-annual cycles are removed, a low-frequency part h_f is fitted with sinusoids of 10, 20 and 40 year periods, and the anomaly h (cm) is converted as

T = −3.69 (h − h_f) − 1.11 h_f (Sv cm⁻¹), [EQUATION 3] [CHECK: signs against Hogg et al., 2015]

with factors derived from NEMO model runs (Hughes et al., 2014) and called preliminary by Hogg et al. (2015). Being the only model-derived quantity of the study, the transport is reported after the index and labelled model-calibrated; it replaces the partial-section surface transport proxy of the discussion version. The conversion is least constrained for trends, and margin sea-level change and sea-ice sampling may alias into the index (Supporting Information S7).

## 2.5 Changes along the LM Gould transect

To compare with Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023), the altimetric cross-track surface velocity and EKE are sampled at 121 points along the mean track of the LM Gould transects (0–850 km from the north), masking points with ice fraction above 0.10 in that month, and analysed in the two coordinate systems of that study: 25 km bins of distance from the north, and pairs of sea-surface-height streamlines, which assign meanders and eddies to their parent jet. Their streamline values (a mean dynamic topography minus 0.60 m plus the sea-level anomaly) are mapped onto our ADT by sampling the monthly ADT where each streamline pair was found on each transect (ADT ≈ 0.62 + 1.04ψ, in m). The in situ trends are recomputed from the archived ADCP velocity at 70–118 m, transport per streamline pair and EKE (Product 3) with the same test; trends over October 2005–April 2019 use annual means for both records.

## 2.6 Along-stream path and tracked eddies

The jet path is tracked monthly as the +0.18 m contour of ADT relative to its monthly band mean, which removes the band-mean part of sea-level rise (Gille, 2014). The path is smoothed at 150 km and divided into 72 bins of 5° of longitude, in which we compute its latitude, sinuosity (path length over zonal length, distinct from curvature), the speed along it and the EKE within 150 km. The two Malvinas Current bins (60–50°W) are excluded (Supporting Information S9); bins where the mean path crosses depths shallower than 3000 m mark the obstacles. Correlations between trend profiles are tested with 2000 circular shifts. Four wake boxes within the ACC band downstream of major topography (75–95°E, 145–175°E, 150–125°W and 55–35°W) test whether the EKE increase concentrates in the wakes. Tracked eddies come from META4.0 (Product 5; Table 1).

## 2.7 Statistics

Trends are Theil–Sen slopes of deseasonalised series (Theil, 1950; Sen, 1968), in absolute units and in percent of the period mean per decade. The slope is divided by its standard deviation under an autoregressive noise model with bootstrap bias-corrected coefficients (Kilian, 1998), and p-values and 95 % intervals are calibrated against 300 trend-free series simulated from that model. Monthly series take the more conservative of two model orders and annual series use AR(1); false-positive rates at the nominal 5 % level are 3–5 % for monthly series and up to 9.5 % for 15 annual values (Supporting Information S2). A single level, p < 0.05, is used; p = 0.050 is reported as at the threshold, and p of about 0.03–0.07 as marginal, because with 300 null series p near 0.05 carries a Monte Carlo uncertainty of about ±0.01. For 1° cells and along-stream bins the false discovery rate (FDR) is controlled at 10 %, equivalent to a global level of about 5 % for correlated fields (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995; Wilks, 2016). Contrasts (e.g. EKE − KE) are trends of differences of mean-normalised series, in percentage points per decade. Because EKE and KE scale with velocity squared and the speed and transport proxies with velocity, we also compare them in energy-equivalent terms (twice the relative trend of a velocity-like quantity). All regions and parameters were fixed before trends were computed; the few post hoc tests are listed in Supporting Information S2.

# 3 Results

## 3.1 Drake Passage: more eddy energy downstream, a band that meanders but does not move

Eddy saturation predicts that stronger winds energise the eddies more than the mean flow and transport; Drake Passage, where in situ observations allow a comparison, is the first test (wind-stress trends are given in Supporting Information S10).

The clearest change lies upstream, where EKE increased over most of the ACC band between the Subantarctic and Polar Fronts (Fig. 1a): KE increases by 12.8 % per decade (6.8–18.7; p = 0.007) and EKE by 15.3 % (6.2–24.4; p = 0.013), in both products (Supporting Information S1). The upstream mean is low, so the absolute changes, 19.2 (10.3–28.2) and 22.5 (9.1–36.0) cm² s⁻² per decade on means of 150 and 147 cm² s⁻², are comparable with the EKE change in the box. The upstream along-stream speed increases by 3.9 % per decade (0.3–7.5; 0.33 cm s⁻¹ per decade on a mean of 8.4 cm s⁻¹), at the threshold (p = 0.050).

Inside the passage, KE (+2.4 % per decade, −2.5 to +7.4; p = 0.29) and the along-stream speed (−0.1 %, p = 0.91) show no detectable trend, while EKE increases by 8.4 % per decade (0.2–16.6; 21.7 cm² s⁻² per decade on a mean of 258 cm² s⁻²), at the threshold (p = 0.050); this trend is not confirmed by the two-satellite product and weakens without the ice mask (Supporting Information S1, S5), so no conclusion rests on it. Downstream, EKE increases by 8.0 % per decade (3.0–13.0; p = 0.010) in the all-satellite product only, and KE shows no detectable trend (+4.3 %, p = 0.13). Between the mean Subantarctic and Polar Fronts, the absolute EKE increase is largest just downstream of the passage, over the western Scotia Sea at 54–46°W, where the flow crosses the North Scotia Ridge (+30 to +47 cm² s⁻² per decade; p < 0.05 in two of four 2° bins, p = 0.05–0.06 in the others); smaller increases are detected upstream at 86–76°W (+22 to +28; p ≤ 0.03 in three bins), and none inside the passage (Fig. 1c).

Along the LM Gould transect, the altimetric trends over the period analysed by Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023), October 2005–April 2019, show the same cross-passage pattern as the ADCP record (Fig. 1e): the cross-track velocity increases between the Subantarctic and Polar Fronts (150–300 km from the north; +3.6 cm s⁻¹ per decade in altimetry, +4.1 in the ADCP velocity at 70–118 m), decreases on the southern flank of the Polar Front (350–550 km; −1.9 in both) and increases near the Southern ACC Front (750–850 km; +2.9 and +3.1). With 15 annual values, only the altimetric zone trend near the Southern ACC Front, seasonally masked by sea ice, is significant (Supporting Information S5). Over 2001–2025, the increase between the fronts persists and becomes detectable (+3.0 cm s⁻¹ per decade, 0.6–5.4; p = 0.02, in both products), whereas the opposing trends on the Polar Front flank (−0.4, p = 0.57) and near the Southern ACC Front (−0.3, p = 0.70) vanish: the compensating pattern of 2005–2019 was partly specific to that period. In streamwise coordinates, which assign meanders and eddies to their parent jets, the surface transport between streamlines changes little (Fig. 1d): over 2001–2025, by −1.7 % per decade in the Subantarctic Front (−3.3 to −0.2; p = 0.03, all-satellite product only) and +0.8 % between the fronts (p = 0.050, at the threshold). This agrees with the conclusion of Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) that the acceleration between the fronts in passage coordinates reflects meandering and eddies rather than a faster jet, although the acceleration of the Subantarctic Front that they found in streamwise coordinates is not seen in the surface transport. EKE in the Subantarctic Front increases in both records over 2005–2019 (+24 % per decade in situ, +19 % in altimetry, neither significant with our test; Supporting Information S5) and by 18 % per decade over 2001–2025 (−1 to +38; p = 0.056; Supporting Information S5); the EKE decrease in the Polar Front reported by Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) is not seen in altimetry (+18 % per decade over 2005–2019, p = 0.11). Along the transect over 2001–2025, surface EKE increases across the Subantarctic Front (up to about 13 % per decade) and south of the Polar Front (up to about 16 %) and changes little between the fronts, but no bin trend is significant except a decrease at the southern end; over 2005–2019, the in situ 0–780 m EKE has a similar structure, with increases at the Subantarctic Front and south of the Polar Front and a decrease near the Polar Front (Supporting Information S5, Fig. S6). Over 70–55°W, the half-power latitude has no trend (−6.8 km per decade, −21.9 to +8.2), but a 25 km shift is detected in only a third of synthetic tests; the streamline through its mean position has not moved (−0.1, −4.5 to +4.3; Supporting Information S3). Monthly half-power points spread over several degrees of latitude upstream of the passage, where the band is weak and broad, and concentrate along narrow paths in the passage and over the Scotia Sea, which nearly coincide in 2001–2015 and 2016–2025 (Fig. 1b); between 86 and 34°W, the mean positions of 2001–2008, 2009–2016 and 2017–2025 differ on average by about 5 km, against month-to-month excursions of about 70 km (standard deviation; Supporting Information S3): the energetic band meanders but has not moved.

## 3.2 Stronger eddies around the ACC

Is the Drake Passage pattern circumpolar, and does the earlier altimetric EKE increase persist? Because the energetic pathway has not moved detectably (Sect. 3.4), the trends below describe changes of the flow in place.

Over the ACC band, EKE increases by 1.98 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.81–3.15; 8.0 % per decade; p = 0.017) and KE by 1.36 cm² s⁻² yr⁻¹ (0.86–1.86; 4.9 % per decade; p = 0.003). In the two-satellite product the EKE trend is about half as large, although the difference between products is not significant (+4.3 percentage points per decade, −2.2 to +10.8; p = 0.12; Supporting Information S1). Much of the rise occurred after 2018, with the highest annual EKE in 2020–2023 in both products and lower values since (Fig. 2c). KE and EKE trends are positive in all sectors, significant for both in the Pacific and Indian and for KE in the Atlantic (EKE p = 0.053; Fig. 2d). Relative trends do not scale with area, but their significance can, because a larger sector averages more independent variability; they do not support a ranking of sectors. The largest absolute increases, mostly not significant at the cell level, lie where mean EKE is largest, downstream of the Kerguelen Plateau, Southeast Indian Ridge and Campbell Plateau (Fig. 2a). Only 2.9 % (KE) and 4.0 % (EKE) of 1° cells reach p < 0.05, no more than expected by chance, and 5 and 18 cells survive FDR control: the signal rests on area averages.

Processed as in the earlier studies, our data give trends of the same order (Supporting Information S6, Fig. S7). With the procedure of Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021), EKE over 35–65°S increases by 5.2 % per decade over 2001–2020 and 7.5 % over 2001–2025, against their 5.1 % over 1993–2020; with the calibrated test, the trend is not significant over 2001–2020 (p = 0.13) and is 6.6 % (1.2–12.0; p = 0.04), a marginal result, over 2001–2025; our integrated EKE level is about twice theirs, for reasons not yet identified. In the boxes of Hogg et al. (2015), EKE increases by 9.0 % per decade (4.9–13.0; p = 0.007) in the Indian and 8.6 % (0.9–16.4; p = 0.04) in the Pacific sector, against their 6.0 ± 1.6 % and 6.4 ± 1.8 %, and the two products bracket the earlier values. With different periods, sampling and reference means, this is consistency, not reproduction.

About 345 tracked eddies are present per day (mean amplitude 7.7 cm, speed radius 41 km). Over January 2001–August 2023 their number (+0.8 % per decade, p = 0.37) and radius (−0.1 %, p = 0.60) show no detectable trend, whereas their amplitude increases by 4.6 % per decade (2.4–6.8; p = 0.007) and their rotational speed by 3.8 % (0.9–6.8; p = 0.02) (Fig. 2e). Within the resolved range, the EKE increase thus reflects stronger rather than more numerous eddies; as the radius is limited by the mapping (fifth percentile 24 km), its stability is weakly informative, and the atlas, built from the all-satellite maps, shares their constellation dependence (Supporting Information S1, S8).

## 3.3 Mean flow and transport

Has the transport changed less than the eddy energy? No detectable trend would be consistent with eddy saturation without testing it.

In Drake Passage, the in situ 0–760 m transport changes by −1.2 % per decade over 2005–2019 (−12.7 to +10.3; p = 0.70), from 15 annual values. Around the ACC, the along-stream speed increases by 2.0 % per decade over the band (p = 0.003; about 2 % in both products), by 3.5 % in the Pacific and 1.8 % in the Atlantic, but not significantly in the Indian sector (Fig. 2b, d). The excess of the relative EKE trend over the KE trend, +3.0 percentage points per decade (−0.1 to +6.1; p = 0.060), is not significant, and in energy-equivalent terms the along-stream speed (4.0 % per decade) rises at about the KE rate (4.9 %). This describes the surface partition between eddies and mean flow and is not a saturation test.

## 3.4 A stationary energetic pathway

A trend in a fixed region could reflect a moving current rather than a changing one, so we ask whether the energetic band has moved; a stationary band does not by itself test eddy saturation.

Averaged over the 900 persistently tracked longitudes, the half-power latitude changes by −0.5 km per decade (−4.2 to +3.1; p = 0.74), although a 25 km shift would have been detected; its interannual excursions of 10–15 km reverse within a few years (Fig. 3b). No sector has a significant trend, and none of the 33 longitude bins of 5° reaches p < 0.05 (Fig. 3c; Supporting Information S3), including the southeast Indian and southeast Pacific sectors where shifts were reported for earlier periods (Kim and Orsi, 2014; Chambers, 2018). Our trends are an order of magnitude below the southward shifts of SSH contours (about 40 km per decade over 1992–2007; Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b). In Drake Passage, where the fronts merge into one energetic envelope, the bound is weak (Sect. 3.1; Fig. 3c). In CMIP6 projections, Schiavon et al. (2025) likewise found the ACC jets largely anchored by topography, with the largest projected displacements in the topographically unconstrained southeast Indian sector.

## 3.5 Standing meanders: no localised response

The standing-meander mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014) implies upstream acceleration, meander flexing and EKE increases localised downstream of topography; detecting these at the surface would make it a candidate explanation, and not detecting them would leave it untested.

Along the jet-core path, EKE increases in 58 of 70 bins (Fig. 3d), by similar amounts over the obstacles (7.0 % per decade on average), in the three bins downstream (7.3 %) and elsewhere (6.9 %); 12 bins reach p < 0.05 and none survives FDR control. The four wake boxes hold 43 % of the band's mean EKE and 45 % of its increase (relative-trend difference +0.1 percentage points per decade, p = 0.95). The jet speed increases in 51 of 70 bins (median +4.1 % per decade; Fig. 3e). At the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge, on the sections of Liu et al. (2026), the jet core accelerates by 0.019 m s⁻¹ per decade (p = 0.01), whereas upstream of the ridge the increase is borderline (p = 0.06). Neither the sinuosity (36 of 70 bins positive; 3 with p < 0.05, none after FDR control) nor the path latitude (35 of 70 positive; 3 isolated bins after control) shows a tendency (Fig. 3c, f). A half-power curvature test at individual meanders was feasible only at the Southeast Indian Ridge, where a 25 km flexing would not be detectable (Supporting Information S4). Across bins, the EKE trend is larger where the jet-speed trend is larger (r = 0.40, p = 0.008); because EKE and the monthly path share the eddies and respond to common mapping changes, this association does not separate cause from effect.

# 4 Discussion and conclusions

Three results emerge from the 2001–2025 altimetric record. First, the eddy field of the ACC has strengthened at the surface: EKE increased by 8 % per decade because existing eddies became more energetic, and the earlier altimetric increase (Hogg et al., 2015; Martínez-Moreno et al., 2021) persists in the reprocessed record with a conservative test. Second, the surface mean flow strengthened as well, so the record shows a joint intensification rather than eddies growing over an unchanged mean flow. Third, the current intensified in place: the energetic pathway has not moved detectably where it is persistently tracked, and no regional shift of the kind reported for 1993–2016 (Chambers, 2018) is detected, a difference that may reflect the period or the method. The magnitude of the EKE increase depends on the constellation by about a factor of two, a difference not significant over 25 years but large enough to quote both products.

These results bear on how the ACC responds to a changing climate and how it is monitored. A stationary pathway is consistent with topographic anchoring of the jets (Schiavon et al., 2025) and with the view that the southward drift of SSH contours largely reflects sea-level rise (Gille, 2014), so warming observed at fixed latitudes need not imply a southward-moving current. Surface EKE is now a detectable trend indicator, but its magnitude depends on the constellation, so monitoring needs a constant-constellation product alongside the all-satellite one; and because the mean flow strengthened too, the along-stream speed and pathway position are complementary indicators.

The absence of a detectable trend in the in situ Drake Passage transport is consistent with eddy saturation (Constantinou and Hogg, 2019; Maddison et al., 2025) but does not test it [PENDING: Antarctic-slope index]. Whether the forcing changed enough to expect a response, and why the EKE increase cannot be attributed to the circumpolar wind on this evidence, is discussed with the wind-stress trends in Supporting Information S10.

Eddy saturation concerns the depth-integrated transport. The strengthening of the surface mean flow neither confirms nor contradicts it: the surface velocity does not represent the flow at depth, the in situ record omits the flow below 760 m (Donohue et al., 2016), and KE includes slow meander variability. The EKE increase does not significantly exceed that of KE (Sect. 3.3). A weak transport trend over 25 years is also not unique to an eddy-saturated equilibrium: the ACC and the global pycnocline adjust to sustained forcing over multidecadal to centennial time scales (Allison et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2011; Samelson, 2011), which the record cannot separate from an equilibrated saturated state.

Of the links in the mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), the surface record shows parts of the first, upstream acceleration: upstream of Drake Passage KE and EKE increase in both products, and at the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge the jet core accelerates within the meander, but in both places the upstream speed increase is only at the threshold or borderline, and the upstream Drake region lies in the Pacific, where speed increases sector-wide. Flexing is not detected and, at 25 km, would not be detectable; the EKE increase is not concentrated over or downstream of the obstacles. In Drake Passage, the persistent acceleration between the fronts in passage coordinates, with little change in the surface transport between streamlines, is consistent with the increased meandering and eddy activity inferred by Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023). Under slowly increasing forcing, the final link would appear as compensation limiting the acceleration rather than as slowing; the surface jet speed increases, so any compensation is at most partial at the surface and cannot be isolated at depth. The uniform increase does not favour barotropic instability in the wakes either (Tansley and Marshall, 2001); without subsurface data, baroclinic-instability indicators cannot be computed, and the surface diagnostics cannot distinguish these pathways.

Altimetry resolves the surface geostrophic flow at wavelengths of about 100–150 km at these latitudes, so the small-scale end of the mesoscale band and the vertical structure of EKE are not observed. The Antarctic-slope index rests on model-derived conversion factors and on the sea-ice sampling of the slope [PENDING: ice-free fraction]. Observations from the Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) mission may help resolve smaller surface scales.

In summary, over 2001–2025 the surface eddy field and mean flow of the ACC strengthened together, with no detectable displacement where the energetic band is persistently tracked, while the in situ Drake Passage transport showed no detectable trend over 2005–2019. These observations are compatible with eddy saturation but do not discriminate it from a joint intensification of the surface flow, because the EKE increase does not significantly exceed that of KE, the full-depth transport is unobserved after 2019 [PENDING: Antarctic-slope index], and the record cannot separate equilibrium from slow adjustment. Whether the pattern persists as the record lengthens remains open.
