# Author comment — reply to Referee #? (general assessment: limitations, resolution, vertical structure)
Manuscript sp-2026-11, "Kinetic Energy Variability and Eddy Saturation in the Southern Ocean", C. Martí-Solana et al.

Dear Referee,

We thank the referee for the careful reading and for the emphasis on the limitations of the data, which the original manuscript did not discuss adequately. We agree that the altimetric record is both the strength and the main limitation of the study, and in the revised version we will make the scope of the dynamics that altimetry can resolve explicit, discuss the surface-only nature of the diagnostics, and tie the frontal analysis to the eddy field through an along-stream analysis of the jet path around the whole ACC, which we have carried out in response to the reviews and summarise below. Below we respond point by point; page/line numbers refer to the discussion version.

**A1. Structure and extension from Drake Passage; surface transport in all sectors; vertical structure.**

We agree that the Drake Passage is where the evidence is most complete, because it is the only place with an independent in-situ transport series. We will follow the referee's suggestion: Drake Passage will open the Results and the Discussion as the observational anchor, followed by the comparison with earlier altimetric estimates and the circumpolar diagnostics as the extension, and we will add transitions that state, for each diagnostic, which question it answers and what result would or would not be consistent with eddy saturation.

Surface transport from altimetry in all sectors: we agree this is a useful addition and have computed it. At each longitude the eastward surface geostrophic velocity is integrated across the fixed ACC band (the band defined by the mean absolute dynamic topography, used for the KE and EKE series), giving a surface transport proxy per unit depth that is averaged over each sector and over the circumpolar band. Preliminary results, with a trend test calibrated for the autocorrelation of the monthly series: the circumpolar proxy increases by 1.6 % per decade (95 % interval 0.9–2.3; p = 0.003) in the all-satellite product and by 1.5 % (0.9–2.1; p = 0.003) in the two-satellite product; by sector, 2.6 % per decade in the Pacific (p = 0.003), 1.8 % in the Atlantic (p = 0.007) and no significant trend in the Indian sector (0.4 %, p = 0.49), with the same values to within 0.2 % in the two-satellite product. Over the same band and period EKE increases by 8.0 % per decade (all-satellite) and 3.8 % (two-satellite), i.e. about five and about 2.5 times faster than the surface transport proxy, and the along-stream speed of the monthly-mean flow by 2.0 % per decade (p = 0.003) in both products. We will present these results in Sect. 3.3 next to the Drake Passage proxies, with all trends (Drake and circumpolar transport proxies, Drake and ACC-band KE and EKE, wind stress) in percent of the mean per decade in a single panel (Fig. 3d), and the two-satellite values in the Supporting Information (S1); the Discussion will state that the circumpolar surface signature of the mean flow is not unchanged, but strengthens several times more slowly than the eddy field, and that a smaller relative trend in the transport proxy than in EKE is not by itself a test of eddy saturation.

Vertical structure: the referee is right that this was not discussed. Altimetry gives surface geostrophic velocities only, and all our transport quantities are proxies: the altimetric one is a surface transport over the permanently ice-free northern part of the section, and the in-situ series of Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023) is the total transport over 0–760 m from repeat ADCP transects, which includes the depth-independent and Ekman contributions within that layer. We do not assume that the surface transport equals the full-depth transport. The argument we can make is weaker and we will state it as such: the surface signature of the mean flow and the 0–760 m transport show no detectable trend over their records (altimetric proxy +2.3 % per decade, 95 % interval −3.7 to +8.2, p = 0.40; in situ −2.8 %, −16.4 to +10.8, p = 0.47), while surface EKE in the passage increases at the limit of significance (+8.4 % per decade, 0.2 to 16.7, p = 0.050) and KE does not (+2.4 %, n.s.), and this is the surface expression expected under eddy saturation, not a demonstration of it. We will add to the Methods and Discussion that the ACC transport has deep and barotropic contributions (Donohue et al., 2016) that neither proxy captures, that the vertical structure of EKE cannot be evaluated from altimetry and that no co-located subsurface EKE record covers 2001–2025, and that this is the main reason why the eddy-saturation interpretation remains a consistency statement rather than a test. We will not attempt to supplement altimetry with reanalysis or model output in this chapter, which is defined around the Copernicus products listed in Table 1.

**A2. Resolution of the altimetry product and the range of eddies evaluated.**

We agree that this is a major methodological point and that it was missing. We will state in the Methods the grid spacing (1/8° for the all-satellite product SEALEVEL_GLO_PHY_L4_MY_008_047, 1/4° for the two-satellite climate product 008_057), and that the effective resolution of the merged maps is set by the mapping, not by the grid: for three-altimeter DUACS maps it was estimated at about 100 km in wavelength at high latitudes and about 200 km at mid-latitudes (Ballarotta et al., 2019) **[add the DT2024 value from the QUID if it differs]**, i.e. the maps resolve the larger part of the mesoscale band and not its small-scale end, which is where the deformation radius of 10–20 km places a substantial fraction of Southern Ocean eddies. EKE in this study is therefore the surface geostrophic EKE at scales resolved by the gridded maps; all trends and comparisons are made within this scale range, which is the same for both products over the full record once the constellation is at least three satellites (the reason for starting in 2001).

To give the referee's question a concrete answer, we have examined the tracked eddies of the AVISO Mesoscale Eddy Trajectory Atlas (META4.0 DT, built from the same all-satellite maps), and propose to include this analysis in the revised version. Preliminary results: in the ACC band over 2001–2023 the tracked eddies have a mean speed radius of 41 km, with a median of about 37 km and a fifth percentile of 24 km, so that the range of eddies evaluated starts at the resolution limit of the maps and eddies smaller than that are not detected; their mean amplitude is 7.7 cm, about 345 are present on an average day and about 330 tracks begin each month. Their radius and number do not change (−0.1 % and +0.8 % per decade, n.s.), while their amplitude (+4.6 %, p = 0.007), rotational speed (+3.8 %, p = 0.02) and the sum of squared amplitudes (+10.9 %, p = 0.007) increase, consistent with the gridded EKE trend over the same months (+8.6 %, p = 0.027). The nonlinearity ratio U/c (rotational speed over translation speed from 7-day displacements, for tracks lasting at least seven days) is 6.9 in the median, 99 % of observations are nonlinear, and the ratio shows no trend (p = 0.97): in that subset both U and c increase, by about 3.5 and 3.2 % per decade. We would present these results in Sect. 3.1, with the eddy series and trends added to Fig. 1 and the generation map and U/c in the Supporting Information, and note that the atlas is built from the all-satellite maps and therefore shares the constellation sensitivity of the all-satellite EKE trend, which is about half as large in the two-satellite product, although the difference between the two trends is not itself significant over 2001–2025 (+4.3 percentage points per decade, 95 % interval −2.2 to +10.8) (Supporting Information S1).

**A3. Relation between the jets' position and EKE; other indicators of instability.**

We agree that the connection was not explained, and we will change the role of the frontal analysis. First, we will no longer present frontal stationarity as evidence for eddy saturation: the half-power analysis will answer the narrower question of whether the energetic ACC pathway has moved while its energy increased, i.e. whether the KE and EKE trends in the fixed band are an intensification of the jets in place or a relocation of the energetic band. The recomputed position trend is −0.5 km per decade for the circumpolar mean, with a 95 % interval of about ±4 km, and synthetic experiments show that a 25 km shift over the record would be detected in every realisation and a 10 km shift in 44 %. We withdraw the claim of a widening of the envelope, because the width metric responds to the amplitude of the KE profile.

Second, we propose to make the link to the eddy field along the jet itself, with an along-stream analysis that we have carried out in response to the reviews. The path of the main jet is tracked each month as the contour of ADT relative to its monthly mean over the ACC band (which removes sea-level rise), at the level whose climatological contour carries the highest mean speed (+0.18 m, on the Subantarctic Front side); it is smoothed at 150 km and divided into 72 bins of 5° of longitude, in each of which we compute the trends of EKE within 150 km of the path, of the jet speed along the path, of the path sinuosity (path length over zonal length, a measure of how much the path meanders, which is distinct from its curvature) and of the path latitude, with false-discovery-rate control across bins (the two bins where the contour follows the Malvinas Current are excluded). Preliminary results: EKE increases in 58 of 70 bins, by similar amounts over the topographic obstacles (7.0 % per decade on average), in the three bins downstream of them (7.3 %) and elsewhere (6.9 %), with 12 bins individually significant and none after false-discovery-rate control; the jet speed increases in 51 of 70 bins (median +3.9 % per decade; 4 individually significant), and at the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge the jet core accelerates by 0.019 m s⁻¹ per decade (p = 0.01, in both products, consistent with Liu et al., 2026), while upstream of the ridge the increase is borderline (p = 0.06); the sinuosity (36 of 70 bins positive, 3 with p < 0.05, none after control) and the latitude of the path (35 of 70 positive, 8 with p < 0.05, 3 after control at isolated bins) show no tendency. Across longitude, the EKE trend is larger where the jet speed increases more (r = 0.40, p = 0.008 from circular shifts of one profile along the path) and where the sinuosity increases more (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), and is unrelated to the latitude trend (r = −0.02, p = 0.95). We will interpret this cautiously: the surface record shows the first ingredient of the standing-meander mechanism of Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014), a faster jet, but the meanders neither flex nor move detectably and the EKE increase is not concentrated over or downstream of the obstacles; the decadal link between the trends of jet speed, sinuosity and EKE is consistent with eddies drawing energy from a strengthening jet, but because EKE and the sinuosity of the monthly path both include the eddies themselves, it does not separate cause from effect, and a change in meander shape may occur below the surface or at scales the maps do not resolve. On monthly time scales, path length and local EKE covary in the same month without a delayed downstream response (Supporting Information S5). We do not have the subsurface data to compute baroclinic-instability indicators (isopycnal slope, Eady growth rate) from observations, and we will say so; the surface diagnostics do not distinguish baroclinic from barotropic instability. We would also report that the EKE increase is not larger in four wake boxes downstream of the major topographic features than over the rest of the band (difference in relative trend +0.1 % per decade, p = 0.95; −0.3 %, p = 0.80, in the two-satellite product), and that eddy generation in the atlas is not concentrated in the wakes.

**Specific comments.**

- *Abstract.* We will rewrite it to state the results (size of the trends, absence of detectable displacement and of transport trends, the decomposition of the EKE increase by the eddy atlas) and drop the half-power method from it; the method will be introduced in Sect. 2.2 with the question it answers.
- *Mean KE not defined.* We will define KE as half the squared speed of the monthly-mean geostrophic velocity, so that EKE is the KE of the deviations from the 2001–2025 mean, and will give both definitions in Sect. 2.1 together with the along-stream speed, noting that slow meander and eddy variability contributes to both KE and EKE and that the along-stream speed is the cleaner measure of the mean flow.
- *SAM.* The statement about "periods consistent with the SAM" was not supported by an analysis and we will not keep it. We have correlated the deseasonalised, detrended ACC-band KE and EKE series with the Marshall (2003) SAM index with the SAM leading by 0–12 months, with significance from phase-randomised surrogates of the index. The correlation is at most 0.15 for KE (lag 0) and 0.09 for EKE, not significant once the lags examined are accounted for (p = 0.12 and 0.24), the 12-month running means are uncorrelated (r = 0.11 and 0.08) and so are the annual means (0.04 and 0.05). We will report this in Sect. 3.1 and describe the interannual variability without attribution; because the relation is not significant, no SAM panel will be added to Fig. 1. The Discussion will note that the lagged, regional response to a single strong SAM event described by Meredith and Hogg (2006) is not expected to appear as a circumpolar correlation over a 25-year record.
