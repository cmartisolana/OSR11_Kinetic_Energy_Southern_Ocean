# Author comment — reply to Referee #? (reject with possibility of resubmission)
Manuscript sp-2026-11, "Kinetic Energy Variability and Eddy Saturation in the Southern Ocean", C. Martí-Solana et al.

Dear Referee,

We thank the referee for the detailed and critical review. We accept the three main criticisms: the manuscript did not state what is new relative to Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) and Hogg et al. (2015); the Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014) mechanism was invoked but not tested; and some of the claimed changes were below what the gridded product can detect. We propose to address each with additional analyses rather than with wording alone; these analyses have been carried out in response to the reviews and their preliminary results are given below. In response to the suggestion to use reanalyses or models, we note that this chapter of the Ocean State Report is built on the Copernicus altimetry products and we intend to keep altimetry as the dataset; instead we will quantify what it can detect, compare the all-satellite with the two-satellite product (Supporting Information S1), and add the tracked-eddy atlas derived from the same maps.

**C1. Novelty relative to Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021).** We agree that the omission was a serious one; we will cite Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) and Hogg et al. (2015) in the Introduction, Results and Discussion. The purpose of the Ocean State Report is to update the observational evidence, and an update is informative whether it confirms, weakens or changes earlier conclusions; we will therefore compare our estimates explicitly with the earlier ones. Processed as in Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021) (EKE integrated over 35–65°S, 12-month running mean, Theil–Sen slope), the all-satellite record gives +5.2 % per decade over 2001–2020 and +7.5 % over 2001–2025, the same order as their +5.1 % per decade for the region south of 35°S over 1993–2020, obtained with an earlier version of the product; with our calibrated test the 2001–2020 trend is not significant (+4.5 % per decade, p = 0.13) and the 2001–2025 trend is (+6.6 %, p = 0.04), so it is the extension of the record that makes the increase detectable with a conservative test. In the three sector boxes of Hogg et al. (2015), EKE increases over 2001–2025 by 9.0 and 8.6 % per decade in the Indian and Pacific sectors in the all-satellite product and by 4.8 and 4.9 % in the two-satellite product, bracketing their 6.0 and 6.4 % per decade from crossover altimetry over a record starting in 1993; over 2001–2012 alone the 95 % intervals are wide (±8 to ±30 % per decade) and only one of the six sector trends (Indian, two-satellite) is significant. Because our record starts in 2001, these comparisons share only part of the earlier periods; the overlap and the reason for the 2001 start (a constellation of at least three satellites) will be stated, and absolute as well as relative trends will be given **[AUTHOR DECISION: add "a comparison over 1993–2025 with the two-satellite product will be given in the Supporting Information" only if the 1993–2000 extraction is done]**. Hogg et al. (2015) also inferred circumpolar transport anomalies from sea level over the Antarctic shelf and slope, with frequency-dependent conversion factors derived from a model (Hughes et al., 2014); **[AUTHOR DECISION: we will compute the corresponding altimetric sea-level index and report it, with its sea-ice coverage, alongside the Drake Passage proxies, without / with the model-based conversion to transport]**. The Introduction will then state what is new: (i) the record is extended to 2025 with the DT2024 reprocessing and the trends are recomputed with a trend test calibrated for the autocorrelation of the monthly series (bias-corrected autoregressive noise, with a false-positive rate verified by simulation), and the local trend maps are, with this test, mostly non-significant at the cell level (2.9 % of KE cells and 4.0 % of EKE cells at p < 0.05, no more than expected by chance; 5 and 18 cells after false-discovery-rate control), so that the signal is the area average, not the map; (ii) the mean flow is examined alongside EKE (preliminary values: along-stream speed +2.0 % per decade in both products; the EKE trend does not significantly exceed the KE trend, +3.0 % per decade with interval −0.1 to +6.1, and the excess vanishes in the two-satellite product); (iii) the constellation dependence of the EKE trend is quantified with the two-satellite climate product (EKE trend about half as large, although the difference between the two trends is not significant over 2001–2025; the divergence grows after 2018), summarised in one sentence of Sect. 3.1 and detailed in the Supporting Information (S1); (iv) the EKE increase is decomposed with the tracked-eddy atlas (unchanged number and radius of eddies; larger amplitude, +4.6 % per decade, and rotational speed, +3.8 %; constant nonlinearity ratio); (v) the Drake Passage comparison with the in-situ series, made over matching latitudes; (vi) the along-stream analysis of the jet described under C2 and the surface transport proxy computed in every sector (circumpolar +1.6 % per decade, about five times slower than EKE; +1.5 % in the two-satellite product). Our conclusion on eddy saturation will be restated as a consistency statement.

**C2. The Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014) mechanism; curvature at specific meanders; upstream acceleration.** We agree that the sector-averaged half-power latitude cannot test the mechanism, and we have carried out the analysis the referee proposes, the curvature of the front at each longitude through time together with the acceleration of the jet upstream of and within the meanders, which we propose to include as a new section (Methods 2.4, Results 3.4, Fig. 4). We do it on the jet-core contour rather than on the half-power points, because the half-power envelope is not found persistently at 69 % of longitudes, which would leave most meanders unsampled. We tested this choice at four meanders selected beforehand from bathymetry and climatology (Southeast Indian Ridge at 140–150°E, Kerguelen, Pacific–Antarctic Ridge, Drake Passage): a single half-power branch can be followed continuously across the meander only at the Southeast Indian Ridge, and there synthetic tests show that the half-power path does not detect a 25 km change in meander amplitude and responds to an intensification of the jet that is stronger on one flank, so that half-power geometry cannot separate flexing from intensification. The path of the main jet is tracked each month as the contour of ADT relative to its monthly mean over the band (which removes sea-level rise), at the level whose climatological contour carries the highest mean speed (+0.18 m, Subantarctic Front side), smoothed at 150 km and divided into 72 bins of 5° of longitude. In each bin and month we compute the sinuosity of the path (path length over zonal length, a measure of how much the path meanders, distinct from its curvature), its mean latitude, the geostrophic speed along it (the speed of the jet core) and the EKE within 150 km of it; trends are computed per bin with false-discovery-rate control across bins, bins where the mean path crosses water shallower than 3000 m mark the obstacles, and the relations between the trend profiles are tested with circular shifts along the path (the two Malvinas Current bins are excluded). Preliminary results: EKE increases in 58 of 70 bins (12 individually significant, none after control) and by similar amounts over the obstacles (7.0 % per decade on average), in the three bins downstream of them (7.3 %) and elsewhere (6.9 %); the jet speed increases in 51 of 70 bins (median +3.9 % per decade; 4 individually significant); the sinuosity shows no tendency (36 of 70 bins positive; 3 with p < 0.05, none after control), nor does the latitude (35 of 70 positive; 8 with p < 0.05, 3 after control, at isolated bins). Across longitude, the EKE trend is larger where the jet accelerates more (r = 0.40, p = 0.008) and where the sinuosity increases more (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), and unrelated to the latitude trend (r = −0.02, p = 0.95). For the upstream acceleration at a particular meander, we have computed the jet-core speed on the sections of Liu et al. (2026) across the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge: it increases by 0.019 m s⁻¹ per decade over the whole meander (0.026 at the Lower Ridge; p = 0.01 in both products), consistent with their result, whereas the increase upstream of the ridge is borderline (p = 0.06); we do not see a slowing of the jet. We will interpret this as follows: the surface record shows the first ingredient of the mechanism, a faster jet, but not the change in meander shape (flexing) through which it would localise the eddy response, and the EKE increase is not concentrated over or downstream of the obstacles; the decadal link between the trends of jet speed, sinuosity and EKE is consistent with eddies drawing energy from a strengthening jet, but because EKE and the sinuosity of the monthly path both include the eddies themselves it does not separate cause from effect, and the flexing step may occur below the surface or at scales the maps do not resolve. On monthly time scales, path length and local EKE covary in the same month without a delayed downstream response (Supporting Information S5). The mechanism will no longer be presented as demonstrated.

**C3. Is altimetry the right dataset; detectability of the width and latitude trends.** The referee's arithmetic is right and we withdraw the envelope-width result: a change of a few kilometres is below what the maps can resolve, and the width metric also responds to the amplitude of the KE profile. For the frontal position we will quantify detectability instead of asserting it; synthetic experiments in which a known shift is imposed on the observed climatological profiles show that a 25 km displacement over 25 years is detected in every realisation and a 10 km displacement in 44 % of them, with about 70 % of the imposed shift recovered. The result we would report is a bound, not a small trend: the recomputed circumpolar position changes by −0.5 km per decade with a 95 % interval of about ±4 km, i.e. a sustained circumpolar shift of about 25 km over the record would have been detected. This is an order of magnitude below the southward shifts of SSH contours reported earlier (about 40 km per decade over 1992–2007, Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b; about 30 km per decade, Gille, 2014); the regional comparison with earlier studies is given under the line 168 comment below. The grid spacing (1/8° all-satellite, 1/4° two-satellite) and the effective resolution of the mapping will be stated in Sect. 2.1. We intend to keep altimetry as the dataset, for the reason given above, and to add the two-satellite product and the tracked-eddy atlas as sensitivity tests; both derive from the same altimetric observations, so their agreement is not an independent validation.

**Minor comments.**

- *Line 34, submesoscale.* We will remove it; the Introduction will refer to the mesoscale only.
- *Lines 50–55, early eddy-saturation papers.* Straub (1993), Hallberg and Gnanadesikan (2001, 2006) and Meredith and Hogg (2006) will be cited where the concept is introduced.
- *Line 83, the 200 cm² s⁻² threshold.* We will give the rationale in Sect. 2.2: the threshold is the median of the 2001–2025 mean KE over the ACC band (195 cm² s⁻²), so that the envelopes are the parts of the band more energetic than its typical level; it, the 25 % peak fraction and the 100 km minimum width were fixed before any trend was computed. The Supporting Information (Sect. S3) will show the sensitivity: thresholds of 150–400 cm² s⁻², peak fractions of 20–50 % and minimum widths of 50–300 km all give p > 0.07 for the circumpolar position trend.
- *Line 108, significance levels.* We agree and will use a single level, p < 0.05, shown by filled symbols, with 95 % confidence intervals, in all figures and in the text; the double stars for p < 0.01 will be removed.
- *Fig. 1 and Martínez-Moreno et al. (2021).* See C1; the comparison will be made in the Results and Discussion.
- *Line 121, downstream of bathymetry.* The 3000 m isobath and the mean jet-core path will be added to the mean EKE map of Fig. 1, and the statement will point to it.
- *Lines 137–138, sector size; regions upstream of and within meanders.* The sector contrast is not a matter of area: the trends are relative (percent per decade) and the uncertainty of a sector trend depends on the temporal autocorrelation of its series rather than on its area, although a larger sector averages out more of the local variability. The regional metrics the referee asks for are covered by the wake-box test (EKE in four boxes downstream of the major topographic features against the rest of the band: difference in relative trend +0.1 % per decade, p = 0.95; −0.3 %, p = 0.80, in the two-satellite product; i.e. a proportionally uniform increase) and by the along-stream analysis in 5° bins, which separates bins over the obstacles, the three bins downstream of them and the rest of the path (C2).
- *Fig. 2a, gaps.* Monthly envelopes are assigned to the envelope detected at the same longitude in the 2001–2025 mean KE field, and only the 900 longitudes where the matched envelope is found in at least 90 % of months are retained, so that the circumpolar and sector statistics are computed over a fixed set of fronts; the gaps upstream of Drake Passage are longitudes without a persistent envelope and are excluded from the averages. The retained longitudes are about 31 % of the band, the gaps lying mainly in the Pacific sector, where the band is weak and broad; this will be stated in Sect. 2.2.
- *Line 168, Schiavon et al. (2025) and spatial variability of the position trends.* The sentence will be corrected, and Schiavon et al. (2025) will be cited for the topographic anchoring of the jets in climate projections. We will compare our trends directly with the earlier estimates over the same regions, in a new panel (Fig. 2c). Preliminary values for 2001–2025, in km per decade (positive northward) with 95 % intervals: 90–170°E −1.6 (−12.9 to +9.6) and 160–90°W +5.6 (−4.9 to +16.2), where Chambers (2018) found −29 and +46 km per decade over 1993–2016; the southeast Indian sector (75–150°E) −1.5 (−9.4 to +6.5), where Kim and Orsi (2014) reported a southward drift over 1992–2011; the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge meander +5.9 (−5.3 to +17.1), where Liu et al. (2026) found about +3 km per decade over 1993–2023; and the circumpolar mean −0.5 (−4.2 to +3.1), against about −40 km per decade for SSH contours over 1992–2007 (Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b) and about −30 km per decade (Gille, 2014). Our result is consistent with the absence of a shift in the zonal-mean Polar Front over 2002–2014 (Freeman et al., 2016) and in the latitude of the mean transport (Gille, 2014); the regional shifts found by Chambers (2018) are not detected over 2001–2025, which suggests that they belonged to the earlier period rather than to a persistent trend. The sector trends are −2.2, −1.2 and +2.0 km per decade (Indian, Pacific, Atlantic; intervals about ±7 km per decade), and trends in 33 longitude bins of 5° will be given in the Supporting Information (S3); none reaches p < 0.05.

With the recalibrated trend test the Drake-box series does show a trend (+8.7 % per decade, 1.4 to 15.9, p = 0.03), whereas the circumpolar band 45–60°S does not (+2.5 %, p = 0.08); both trends will be shown, with those of the transport proxies, KE and EKE, in percent of the mean per decade in a single trend panel (Fig. 3d), and the Results will state them.

- *Reanalysis or eddy-permitting models instead of altimetry.* This is an observational chapter of the Ocean State Report built on the Copernicus Marine Service products of Table 1, and model or reanalysis EKE trends carry their own dependence on the forcing products; we therefore keep altimetry as the dataset and, instead, quantify what it can detect (synthetic shift experiments, the two-satellite product in the Supporting Information, cell-level significance with false-discovery-rate control) and state the limits in a dedicated paragraph. The outlook will mention higher-resolution products, including those derived from SWOT, as the way to resolve what the present maps cannot.
