# Author comment — reply to Referee #? (David Marshall)
Manuscript sp-2026-11, "Kinetic Energy Variability and Eddy Saturation in the Southern Ocean", C. Martí-Solana et al.

Dear Prof. Marshall,

We thank the referee for the constructive review and for the references, which we will include in the Introduction and Discussion.

**B1. Connection between frontal positions and eddy saturation.** We agree. Frontal displacement changes the circumpolar transport only through the change of the Coriolis parameter along the displacement, which is small, and the sentence quoted from the abstract overstated what the frontal analysis can show. We will remove "direct observational evidence" and the causal link between frontal stationarity and eddy saturation. We propose to retain the half-power analysis for a narrower purpose: to show that the energetic ACC pathway has not moved detectably while its KE and EKE increased (recomputed circumpolar position trend −0.5 km per decade, 95 % interval about ±4 km, with synthetic tests showing that a 25 km shift would have been detected), so that the KE and EKE trends in the fixed band are an intensification of the jets in place rather than a relocation. A stable circumpolar position does not by itself constrain the shape of individual standing meanders, which we examine separately (B4). The bound is an order of magnitude below the southward shifts of SSH contours reported for the 1990s and 2000s (about 40 km per decade over 1992–2007, Sokolov and Rintoul, 2009b; about 30 km per decade, Gille, 2014), as expected from the poleward bias of those contours under sea-level rise, and agrees with the absence of a shift in the latitude of the mean transport (Gille, 2014) and in the zonal-mean Polar Front over 2002–2014 (Freeman et al., 2016). The Methods and Discussion will state explicitly that the frontal analysis does not test the transport response. We withdraw the claim of a widening of the frontal envelope (the width metric responds to the amplitude of the profile, and the change was below the resolution of the maps).

**B2. "Circumpolar transport" oversold; proxies.** We agree and will apply the wording throughout: the altimetric quantity is a surface transport proxy (defined in Sect. 2.3 as the surface geostrophic velocity normal to the section integrated over the points free of sea ice in every month; these are the northern 53 of 121 section points, north of about 58.5°S, so that it is a partial-section index), the in-situ series is the total (absolute) transport over 0–760 m from the repeat ADCP transects of Gutierrez-Villanueva et al. (2023), which includes the depth-independent and Ekman contributions within that layer, which will be stated in Sect. 2.3, and both will be referred to as Drake Passage transport proxies; neither captures the deep (below 760 m) and barotropic contributions (Donohue et al., 2016). Fig. 3 will be titled "Transport proxies and eddy kinetic energy", its time-series panel will be labelled "Transport proxies", and the Results and Discussion will use "transport proxies" throughout. To make the proxies comparable with each other and with EKE, all trends will be given in percent of the mean per decade, with 95 % intervals, in a single panel (Fig. 3d). Preliminary values: Drake altimetric proxy +2.3 % per decade (−3.7 to +8.2; p = 0.40); in situ 0–760 m transport −2.8 % (−16.4 to +10.8; p = 0.47); Drake-domain EKE +8.4 % (0.2 to 16.7; p = 0.050) and KE +2.4 % (n.s.); eastward wind stress over the Drake box +8.7 % (1.4 to 15.9) and over 45–60°S +2.5 % (n.s.). In response to another referee we have also computed the surface transport proxy around the whole ACC (integral of the eastward surface geostrophic velocity across the fixed band): it increases by 1.6 % per decade (0.9–2.3; p = 0.003), with 2.6 % in the Pacific, 1.8 % in the Atlantic and 0.4 % (n.s.) in the Indian sector, i.e. about five times more slowly than ACC-band EKE (+8.0 %; KE +4.9 %); these values will be shown in the same panel. The two-satellite values (circumpolar proxy +1.5 % per decade) will be given in the Supporting Information (S1).

**B3. Böning et al. (2008) and adjustment time scales.** We will cite Böning et al. (2008) in the Introduction and add a Discussion paragraph stating that a weak transport trend over 25 years is not unique to an eddy-saturated equilibrium, because the adjustment of the ACC and of the global pycnocline to sustained forcing takes multidecadal to centennial time scales (Allison et al., 2011; Jones et al., 2011; Samelson, 2011); the observations cannot distinguish a fully equilibrated saturated state from a slowly adjusting circulation, and we will restate our conclusion as a consistency statement.

**B4. Baroclinic instability versus topographic wakes and standing meanders.** We agree that the attribution to Ekman steepening was not supported, and we will remove it; the Introduction will present baroclinic instability as one contributing mechanism and name the barotropic route through topographic wakes (Tansley and Marshall, 2001) and changes in the standing meanders as alternatives. Two analyses carried out in response to the reviews bear on the alternative the referee suggests (preliminary results). (i) A localisation test: four wake boxes downstream of the major topographic features (Kerguelen, Southeast Indian Ridge–Macquarie, Pacific–Antarctic Ridge, Drake), fixed from the bathymetry and the climatological EKE before any trend was computed, contain 43 % of the band EKE and 45 % of its increase; the difference between their relative EKE trend and that of the rest of the band is +0.1 % per decade (p = 0.95; −0.3 %, p = 0.80, in the two-satellite product), so the EKE increase is proportionally uniform along the band and not concentrated downstream of topography, although the trend maps show the largest absolute increases downstream of Kerguelen, along the Southeast Indian Ridge and south of New Zealand. (ii) An along-stream analysis of the jet: the path of the main jet is tracked monthly as a contour of ADT relative to its monthly mean (+0.18 m level, Subantarctic Front side), smoothed at 150 km and divided into 72 bins of 5° of longitude, and in each bin we compute the trends of EKE within 150 km of the path, of the jet speed along the path, of the path sinuosity (path length over zonal length, a measure of how much the path meanders, distinct from its curvature) and of its latitude, with false-discovery-rate control across bins. EKE increases in 58 of 70 bins and by similar amounts over the obstacles (7.0 % per decade on average), in the three bins downstream of them (7.3 %) and elsewhere (6.9 %); the jet speed increases in 51 of 70 bins (median +3.9 % per decade), and at the Pacific–Antarctic Ridge the jet core accelerates by 0.019 m s⁻¹ per decade (p = 0.01, in both products, consistent with Liu et al., 2026), with a borderline increase upstream of the ridge (p = 0.06); neither the sinuosity (36 of 70 bins positive, none significant after control) nor the latitude of the path (35 of 70 positive, 3 isolated bins after control) shows a tendency. Across longitude, the EKE trend is larger where the jet accelerates more (r = 0.40, p = 0.008, from circular shifts) and where the sinuosity increases more (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), and unrelated to the latitude trend (r = −0.02, p = 0.95). We will therefore say that the surface record shows the first ingredient of the Thompson and Naveira Garabato (2014) mechanism, a faster jet, but not the change in meander shape through which it would localise the eddy response; the decadal link between jet speed, sinuosity and EKE is consistent with eddies drawing energy from a strengthening jet, but because EKE and the sinuosity of the monthly path both include the eddies themselves it does not separate cause from effect. On monthly time scales, path length and local EKE covary in the same month without a delayed downstream response (Supporting Information S5). The surface diagnostics cannot distinguish whether the instability is baroclinic or barotropic, and the Discussion will say so; the uniform increase does not favour a wake-localised barotropic pathway either.

**B5. Lines 80–81.** We agree and will correct this. With the recomputed trends neither transport proxy shows a significant trend (altimetric +2.3 % per decade, p = 0.40; in situ −2.8 %, p = 0.47) and the two are consistent with each other within their uncertainties; the reference to opposite signs will be removed. We will also compare the two proxies over the same latitudes. The altimetric proxy covers only the permanently ice-free northern part of the section (north of about 58.5°S), and its month-to-month and interannual variations follow the in-situ transport over that same part (r = 0.44 for coincident transects, r = 0.72 for annual means over 2005–2019; r = 0.54 and 0.67 for a section along the mean ADCP track), but not the in-situ transport over the full section (r = 0.05 and 0.11), because the in-situ transports north and south of 58.5°S vary in opposition (r = −0.55). The altimetric proxy is therefore a partial-section index; it does not recover the transport of the southern fronts, and its agreement with the full-section transport is limited to the absence of a trend.
